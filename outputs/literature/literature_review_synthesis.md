**Methodology (of the reviewed corpus).** Method counts: **8 cluster analysis, 11 PCA, 1 t-SNE, 5 combined** (e.g. t-SNE + k-means; cluster analysis + PCA). PCA reporting parameters tabulated per study: KMO thresholds used range from **=0.53 (Fernandez-Navarro) through >0.5, >0.6, >0.7, >0.8, up to >0.9 (Lopez-Valenciano)**; sphericity via Bartlett's test; rotation almost always **Varimax**; factor-retention rule almost always **eigenvalue >1** (Wedding et al. used **>1.2**); loading cut-offs **>0.6** (most) or **>0.7** (Fernandez-Navarro). Clustering algorithms: k-means (most), hierarchical (Ward's method; Louvain and UPGMA in Diquigiovanni), GMM (He et al., with **AIC & BIC** for k), two-step clustering (Wedding et al., BIC-based automatic clustering + silhouette validation). Preprocessing: z-score standardisation reported in De França, Gollan, Branquinho; "Yes" without detail in Moffatt and He; NR in Clavijo and Yi.

**Evaluation metrics and values reported.** Variance explained by PCA solutions across the corpus: **51.00%** (Wedding, 9 factors, rugby), **54.23%** (Wedding et al.8), **55.00% home / 54.20% away** (Kong), **64.29%** (Gómez, 8 factors), **74.40%** (Zhou, 7 factors), **77.79%** (Lopez-Valenciano, 2 dimensions), **78.98%** (Martín-Castellanos, 7 factors), **79.60%** (Lago-Peñas, 5 factors), **79.74%** (Castellano, 2 factors), **83.01%** (Ruan et al.33, 7 factors), **84.81%** (Plakias et al.42, 19 factors), **85.17%** (Ruan et al.21, 8 factors), **87.54%** (Fernandez-Navarro, 6 factors). Other reported values: Wedding's discriminant analysis achieved **81% win–loss classification accuracy**; Ruan et al.21's stepwise regression raised **AUC from 72.90% to 79.83%** and explained **41.6% of variance in opponents' xG**, with "receive a dangerous situation" the strongest factor (**31.3% of variance**); Lopez-Valenciano's 2-D PCA explained **77.8%** of variance in league ranking (dim 1 = 58.5% match dominance/offensive organisation; dim 2 = 19.3% defensive efficiency and reduced long passes); Plakias et al.42's first four factors alone accounted for **49.35%**; Ruan et al.33 found deep-zone defence alone accounted for **31.92%**. Cluster validity indices reported in the corpus: **ARI** (Diquigiovanni), **AIC & BIC** (He), **COA / coefficient of unalikeability** (He), **elbow method** (Moffatt), **silhouette** (Wedding et al.8). Gu et al. report that only **2 of 9** clustering studies explicitly described the criterion for choosing k, and only **7 of 11** PCA studies reported Bartlett's test (10 of 11 reported KMO).

**Key findings.**
1. PCA is the most frequently applied technique (**44%** of included studies), typically combined with k-means.
2. Research is **heavily concentrated on soccer (84%)**; rugby and basketball are underrepresented.
3. Marked heterogeneity in feature selection, preprocessing and reporting undermines reproducibility and comparability.
4. The methods map onto three analytical tasks: **clustering → discrete style recognition; PCA → extraction of interpretable core tactical factors (e.g. "directness", "pressing intensity"); t-SNE → visualisation and similarity mapping**.
5. Practical applications are explicitly listed: (i) game-strategy development, (ii) targeted training design, and (iii) **player recruitment and lineup optimisation** — "recommend players whose individual characteristics align with a team's dominant playing style… in a possession-based system that emphasizes controlled build-up rather than long balls, recruitment efforts may prioritize technically proficient centre-backs and defensive midfielders" (p. 18).

**Stated limitations.** (a) Conceptual ambiguity of "playing style" — "the absence of a universally accepted definition impedes cross-study comparisons" (p. 17), citing Hewitt et al. and Plakias et al.; (b) low methodological transparency (missing KMO/Bartlett/k-selection reporting); (c) limited access to fine-grained spatiotemporal tracking data — technical-tactical data alone "provide insufficient resolution to quantify team-level patterns with precision" (p. 17); (d) t-SNE's parameter sensitivity and computational demands; (e) sport imbalance. Recommendations: adopt **Rojas-Valverde et al. (2021)'s 21-item quality checklist for PCA in sports science**, and Golalipour et al.'s general clustering guidelines; establish a shared operational definition; and pursue "hybrid approaches that combine machine learning with expert input" (p. 17).

**Direct relevance to our project + what it justifies.**
- This is our **methodological anchor**. It licenses, with a systematic-review citation, our core pipeline choice: *dimensionality reduction of many performance indicators into interpretable tactical factors, then clustering/profiling*.
- Cite it to justify: PCA/factor analysis as the standard style-extraction method (44% prevalence); Varimax rotation; eigenvalue>1 retention; loading cut-off ≥0.6; KMO and Bartlett reporting; z-score standardisation before clustering; k-means as the default clustering algorithm; and reporting elbow + silhouette for k.
- Cite it to justify our **methods-reporting rigour** as a contribution: Gu et al. explicitly identify non-reporting of k-selection (7/9 studies) and Bartlett (4/11) as a field-level weakness. Following the Rojas-Valverde 21-item checklist is a defensible, citable quality claim for our CRISP-DM *Data Preparation* and *Modeling* chapters.
- Cite it to justify the **explicit knowledge layer**: the review names the absence of a shared operational definition of playing style as the field's central conceptual gap, and recommends hybrid ML + expert-input approaches. That is precisely the niche of our Football Knowledge Layer.
- Cite it for the **recruitment/fit application** (practical application iii) — the literature already frames style→player-fit as the obvious downstream use, but as an *implication*, not as an implemented model. That framing supports our contribution being an operationalisation of something the field says is useful but has not built.

### 1.2 Plakias et al. (2025), JPES — Overall tactical identity of European soccer teams

**Full citation.** Plakias S, Armatas V, Mprotsis T, Kyranoudis A, Gu L, Xylomenos F, Michailidis Y. *What playing styles best represent the overall tactical identity of European soccer teams?* Journal of Physical Education and Sport (JPES), Vol. 25 (issue 11), Art 248, pp. 2249–2257, November 2025. DOI: 10.7752/jpes.2025.11248. Online ISSN 2247-806X; p-ISSN 2247-8051. Published online 30 November 2025; accepted 15 November 2025.

**Research question / objective.** "to identify and describe the dominant playing styles that define the overall tactical profiles of professional soccer teams in modern European soccer" (p. 2250), via two questions: (1) can European teams be objectively grouped into clusters based on tactical behaviour across multiple game situations? (2) what tactical characteristics distinguish each identified style?

**Data source.** **174 teams from 11 European leagues, 2021–2022 season.** Initial dataset **5,998 observations** (one per team per match); the mean of each of the 19 variables was computed per team, yielding a final dataset of **174 observations × 19 continuous variables**. Unit of analysis = **team-season**. Underlying data provider: **InStat Ltd** (written informed consent obtained 8 November 2022); the 19 latent variables are re-used from Plakias, Kokkotis, et al. (2023). Ethics approval: University of Thessaly, 12 October 2022. Team-league lists and cluster memberships are in Supplementary Materials at https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.16867187.

**Features / variables used — the validated 19-dimension set (verbatim from Table 1, with both poles).** This is the most directly reusable artefact in the entire corpus for our ontology.

| # | Game situation (latent variable) | High factor score | Low factor score |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Build up phase | Possession-based | Direct-based with many long passes |
| 2 | Transition game | Many transitions | Few transitions |
| 3 | Attacking transition | Counterattack | Positional attack |
| 4 | Defensive transition | Opponent's counterattack | Opponent's positional attack |
| 5 | Aerial game | Game in the air | Game on the ground |
| 6 | Type of attack | Set pieces attack | Open play attack |
| 7 | Crossing | Many crosses | Few crosses |
| 8 | Type of opponent's attack | Open play defense | Set pieces defense |
| 9 | Defensive blocks | Mid block | Low block |
| 10 | Press | High press | Deep press |
| 11 | Individual defending actions | Many individual defending actions | Few individual defending actions |
| 12 | Width of creative phase | Center attack | Wide attack |
| 13 | Effective game | More interruptions and duels | More possession from one or the other team |
| 14 | Individual attacking actions | Many individual attacking actions | Few individual attacking actions |
| 15 | Tendency to create final attempts | Strong tendency | Low tendency |
| 16 | Passing tempo | Low passing tempo | High passing tempo |
| 17 | Defending aggressively | Low defensive aggressiveness | High defensive aggressiveness |
| 18 | Attacking aggressively | High attacking aggressiveness | Low attacking aggressiveness |
| 19 | Offside trap | More frequent adoption of the offside trap | Less frequent adoption |

Note the counter-intuitive polarity of #16 (high score = *low* tempo) and #17 (high score = *low* aggressiveness) — a trap to avoid if we reuse these definitions.

**Methodology.** All 19 variables **z-standardised by variable** (mean 0, SD 1) prior to clustering. **Hierarchical cluster analysis**, **Ward's method** linkage ("minimizes the total within-cluster variance"), **squared Euclidean distance** dissimilarity. The dendrogram suggested two clusters each containing two sub-clusters; clustering was therefore run in stages — first extract 2 clusters (A, B), then re-run within each to extract 2 sub-clusters each (A→C,D; B→E,F). **Discriminant analysis run three times** (A–B, C–D, E–F) to identify which of the 19 variables significantly differentiated groups; then **independent-samples t-tests** on the significant variables to quantify magnitude. Normality assessed by Shapiro–Wilk, Q–Q plots, histograms, skewness and kurtosis. Effect sizes via **Cohen's d** with thresholds 0.2 negligible / 0.5 small / 0.8 medium / 1.2 large / 2.0 huge. Software SPSS 29.0; α = 0.05. Graphical style summary built only from differences with **Cohen's d > 0.8**.

**Evaluation metrics and values reported.** Cluster sizes: A = 103, B = 71; C = 28, D = 75; E = 37, F = 34. **Discriminant-analysis correct classification rates: 90.8% (A vs B), 100% (C vs D), 97.2% (E vs F).** Wilks' λ and p reported for all 19 variables in each of the three analyses (e.g. Build up phase λ = 0.764 A–B, 0.731 C–D, 0.697 E–F, all p < .001; Press λ = 0.771 / 0.780 / 0.935). Largest effect sizes: Transition game E–F **d = 2.208**; Defending aggressively C–D **d = 1.855**; Aerial game C–D **d = 1.588**; Build up phase C–D **d = −1.351**; Tendency to create final attempts A–B **d = −1.386**.

**Key findings.** Four interpretable overall styles:
- **Style C** — traditional British direct play: direct build-up, frequent aerial duels, set-piece reliance. 10 of 20 English and 6 of 12 Scottish teams fall here.
- **Style D** — aggressive ground-based directness ("Spanish *furia*"): more retention than C but still direct, on the ground, with high defensive aggressiveness. Exemplars named: **Atlético Madrid, Athletic Bilbao, Sevilla** plus 12 other Spanish teams.
- **Style E** — controlled possession + rapid counterattacks. Exemplars named: **Atalanta and Liverpool**.
- **Style F** — structured positional play, high ball retention (tiki-taka / Sarriball). Exemplars named: **Barcelona, Bayern Munich, Manchester City, Napoli, Chelsea, Lazio, Juventus**, plus **Arsenal** (coached by Mikel Arteta, "who had previously served as Guardiola's assistant at Manchester City").

The authors explicitly note that finer clustering "might have revealed additional distinct playing styles… catenaccio or gegenpressing may currently be embedded within broader styles like D or E" (p. 2253–2254), with gegenpressing characterised as "intense pressing with numerous individual defensive duels, and quick counterattacks… prominent features of Style E" (citing Bauer & Anzer, 2021).

**Stated limitations.** (1) Single season (2021–22) — cannot capture temporal fluctuation from coaching-staff, roster or strategy changes; (2) European teams only; (3) the 19 game situations omit qualitative dimensions such as team cohesion, decision-making quality, psychological resilience; (4) no modelling of dynamic in-match adaptation (ecological dynamics / complex adaptive systems); one aggregated season-long style per team. Future work suggested: multi-season stability, non-European leagues, contextual match variables (match status, opponent quality, home/away), and linking style to outcomes (points, goals, xG).

**Direct relevance to our project + what it justifies.** This is the **single most important paper for us after Gu et al.**
- **It hands us a validated, published, soccer-specific 19-dimension tactical vocabulary with explicit bipolar semantics.** This is exactly the "tactical dimensions" tier of our Football Knowledge Layer, and we can adopt it wholesale with a citation rather than inventing dimensions.
- It supplies **the crucial manager-adjacent evidence**: teams coached by Guardiola-lineage managers (Man City, Bayern, Barcelona, Arsenal-under-Arteta) empirically co-cluster in Style F, and **Liverpool co-clusters with Atalanta in Style E** (possession + fast transition), while gegenpressing is stated to be *embedded within* Style E rather than separately resolved. That is a directly citable empirical anchor for our Guardiola vs Klopp contrast — and simultaneously the clearest evidence that **team-level clustering is too coarse to isolate a manager's philosophy**, which is our gap.
- Cite it to justify: hierarchical clustering with Ward's method + squared Euclidean distance on z-scored features; **staged/hierarchical decomposition of styles** (coarse → sub-styles), which maps beautifully onto CRISP-DM iterative modelling; **discriminant analysis + Wilks' λ as an internal validation of cluster separability** (a much stronger validity argument than silhouette alone); **effect-size thresholds (Cohen's d > 0.8) as the filter for what counts as a defining characteristic of a profile** — we should use exactly this device to decide which dimensions define each manager's profile; and season-mean aggregation of per-match observations.
- Cite it to justify our framing: it states the field's problem as the lack of "soccer-specific interpretability" in AI style methods, because prior methods "focused on structural/computational pattern recognition rather than on the tactical description of the defining features of each style" (p. 2250). That sentence is the mission statement of our knowledge-informed framework.
- It also explicitly recommends the downstream use we intend: "scouting departments and player agents can assess player–team fit" by "matching individual strengths with the tactical requirements of each style (e.g., aerial proficiency in Style C, technical stability under possession in Style F, or transition speed in Style E)" (p. 2255). Again — proposed, not implemented.

### 1.3 Zhong, Xu, Zhu, Diaz-Cidoncha Garcia, Gómez Ruano & Yi (2026), Biology of Sport

**Full citation.** Zhong Y, Xu Y, Zhu K, Diaz-Cidoncha Garcia J, Gómez Ruano MÁ, Yi Q. *Data-driven classification of playing styles and match outcome prediction in UEFA Champions League teams.* Biol Sport. 2026;43:575–586. DOI: https://doi.org/10.5114/biolsport.2026.154944. Received 2025-06-02; accepted 2025-09-30; published 2025-11-03. Funding: Social Science Planning Funds of Liaoning Province, Grant No. L24CTY004.

**Research question / objective.** To build "an integrated, nonlinear, and explainable machine learning framework capable of simultaneously classifying playing styles and predicting match outcomes" (p. 576), and to test "whether performance metrics contribute differently to success in possession-based versus counterattacking teams".

**Data source.** UEFA Champions League, **2017/2018 to 2023/2024 seasons**; **869 matches, n = 1,738 observations** (team-match). Provider: **WhoScored.com, sourcing OPTA Sportsdata**, with inter-operator reliability cited as **kappa > 0.90** (Liu et al., 2013). Variables with excessive zero values were excluded, leaving **30 technical performance variables**. Unit of analysis = team-match. Cluster sizes: Cluster 0 n = 1,106; Cluster 1 n = 632. No ethical approval required.

**Features / variables used (all 30, verbatim from Table 1 with cluster means, Cluster 0 / Cluster 1).** Possession (%) 55.6/40.1; Touches 750.2/570.5; Pass Success (%) 85.7/76.5; Accurate Passes 486.5/291.7; Key Passes 10.9/7.9; Total Passes 588.4/397.4; Crosses 17.7/13.2; Short passes 517.5/323.7; Dribbles Won 10.8/8.6; Dribbles Attempted 18.9/16.4; Dribble Success (%) 57.2/52.5; Dispossessed 9.6/9.1; Open Play 10.3/7.3; Set Piece 3.2/2.7; 18-yard Box (%) 58/52.5; Aerials Won (%) 52.1/46.4; Successful Aerials 12.2/13.5; Defensive Aerials 11.9/13.5; Offensive Aerials 11.1/15.6; Successful Tackles 16.2/17.8; Tackles Attempted 25.6/28.7; Clearances 14/22.4; Interceptions 10.5/12.2; Was dribbled 9.5/11; Cards per Foul (%) 15.6/21.5; Out of Box (%) 35.3/41.4; Long Balls 51.4/59.4; Tackle Success (%) 63.8/62.5; Corners Accuracy (%) 45.5/47.6; Fouls 11.8/11.8.

Retained key indicators after triple feature selection (Table 2): **possession-based style** — Possession, Accurate Passes, Key Passes, Dribbles Won, Aerials Won, Offensive Aerials, Defensive Aerials, Tackle Success, Clearances, Out of Box, Crosses, Cards per Foul (12 KPIs). **Counterattacking style** — Possession, Key Passes, Defensive Aerials, Clearances, Open Play, Out of Box, Crosses, Cards per Foul (8 KPIs).

**Methodology.** (1) **Z-score standardisation**. (2) **Autoencoder** (symmetric encoder–decoder neural network) trained to compress the 30 standardised variables into a **2-D latent representation**; motivation stated explicitly — "PCA is a linear method and often fails to capture nonlinear relationships" (p. 576). (3) **K-means** on the encoded features; k chosen by maximum **silhouette score** over a range of k, giving **k = 2**. (4) **Feature selection by intersection of three methods**: LASSO (λ_min via 10-fold cross-validation), Boruta (random-forest shadow-attribute comparison, 100 iterations), and XGBoost importance; only KPIs selected by *all three* were retained. (5) Outcome recoded as binary **WIN vs NOWIN** (draw or loss), justified because draws are stochastic, low-frequency, and induce class imbalance and model instability. (6) Three classifiers per style: **SVM (RBF kernel; gamma and cost by grid search + cross-validation), KNN (k tested 1–100 by 10-fold CV, selected on highest AUC), LightGBM (learning rate 0.02, num_leaves 31, early stopping after 30 iterations)**. 70/30 random train/test split. (7) **SHAP** values computed on the selected model for interpretability. (8) **Ablation study** with fixed random seed comparing E0 (no feature selection, all raw features) vs E1 (full method: autoencoder + multi-method feature selection). Software: R 4.4.3 and Python 3.10.9.

**Evaluation metrics and values reported.** AUC and F1 with 95% CIs (Table 3).
Possession-based: SVM E0 AUC 0.768 (0.742–0.794) / F1 0.743; **SVM E1 AUC 0.783 (0.759–0.807) / F1 0.757** (best); KNN E0 0.708/0.655, KNN E1 0.735/0.718; LightGBM E0 0.759/0.693, LightGBM E1 0.774/0.671.
Counterattacking: SVM E0 AUC 0.746/F1 0.732; SVM E1 0.701/0.840; KNN E0 0.692/0.645; **KNN E1 AUC 0.726/F1 0.854** (best F1); LightGBM E0 0.717/0.683; **LightGBM E1 0.741 AUC but F1 collapses to 0.235** — the authors note LightGBM's "high sensitivity to feature selection".
LASSO λ_min = 0.0113 (possession, 18 non-zero predictors) and 0.0095 (counterattacking, 18 predictors); Boruta retained 21 (possession) and 16 (counter); XGBoost 20 and 20.

**Key findings.** Cluster 0 = possession-based (higher possession, key passes, open-play shots); Cluster 1 = counterattacking (higher successful tackles, clearances, defensive aerial duels). **The same KPI has opposite associations with winning depending on style** — the paper's headline result. Possession-based teams: top-3 SHAP features Key Passes, Crosses, Possession; positively associated with winning = Crosses, Out of Box shots, Cards per Foul, Offensive Aerials; **negatively** associated = Key Passes, Possession, Aerials Won, Clearances, Accurate Passes, Tackle Success, Dribbles Won, Defensive Aerials. Counterattacking teams: top-3 SHAP features Clearances, Key Passes, Out of Box shots; positive = Clearances, Key Passes, Open Play shots, Possession, Defensive Aerials; negative = Out of Box shots, Crosses, Cards per Foul. Common predictors across both styles: Possession, Key Passes, Defensive Aerials, Clearances, Out of Box shots, Crosses, Cards per Foul — "however, their impact on match outcomes differed significantly across the two styles" (p. 583).

**Stated limitations.** Static aggregated match data cannot capture in-game tactical shifts or momentum; contextual factors (opponent strength, match location, player availability) not incorporated; **nesting effects not modelled** (repeated team observations across seasons violate independence — hierarchical/mixed-effects models recommended); binary win/non-win limits tactical relevance; no direct performance comparison with prior frameworks was conducted. Future work: event-level/time-series data, contextual variables, mixed-effects models, more granular outcomes, extension to women's football.

**Direct relevance to our project + what it justifies.**
- **The strongest citation in the corpus for style-conditional feature importance.** It empirically demonstrates that a metric's value is not intrinsic but conditional on the tactical system — which is the entire logic of player–manager *compatibility*. If crosses help possession teams win and hurt counterattacking teams, then "is this player good?" is unanswerable without a philosophy, and "does this player fit Guardiola?" is the right question. Cite this as the empirical foundation of our research question.
- Cite it to justify **SHAP as the explainability mechanism** for our compatibility model, and for reporting SHAP *direction* as well as magnitude.
- Cite it to justify **multi-method feature-selection intersection (LASSO ∩ Boruta ∩ XGBoost)** as a robustness device — a defensible, cheap addition to our Data Preparation phase.
- Cite it to justify **silhouette-maximisation for k** and z-scoring before clustering, and **AUC + F1 with 95% CIs** as our classification reporting standard.
- Cite it to justify **running an ablation** (with vs without our Football Knowledge Layer) — E0/E1 is a directly transferable design for demonstrating that the knowledge layer adds value rather than merely decorating the model.
- Warning to inherit: it is the paper that shows the **nesting problem** (repeated team/manager observations across seasons). Our manager profiles will be built from many matches of the same team-season — we must acknowledge or model this.
- Contrarian note worth citing honestly: this paper argues **against** PCA on nonlinearity grounds and uses an autoencoder. Our project should acknowledge this trade-off and defend PCA/factor analysis on **interpretability** grounds (which is exactly the argument Plakias 2025 and Bolin & Vesterlind make).

### 1.4 Bolin & Vesterlind (2025), Uppsala University MSc thesis — *From Patterns to Playbooks*

**Full citation.** Bolin N, Vesterlind E. *From Patterns to Playbooks: Identifying offensive and defensive team playing styles from possession chains with machine learning and its possible practical applications in management of football teams.* UPTEC I 25002, Degree Project 30 ECTS, June 2025. Master's Programme in Industrial Engineering and Management, Faculty of Science and Technology, Uppsala University, Uppsala, Sweden. Supervisor: Prof. David J. T. Sumpter; subject reader: Kaveh Amouzgar; examiner: Nina Kivinen. **DOI not stated in PDF.** (Note: Plakias et al. 2025 cite this work as "Bolin, N., & Elliot, V. (2025)" — an error in their reference list; the title page reads *Niklas Bolin & Elliot Vesterlind*.)

**Research question / objective.** Two research questions (p. 3): "1. What offensive and defensive playing styles can be identified through machine learning analysis of possession chains derived from event and tracking data? 2. What practical applications for football team management can be derived from such playing style analysis?" The purpose is explicitly dual: methodological *and* application-focused, "to contribute to bridging the gap between methodological and application-focused research in playing style analysis" (p. 3).

**Data source.** **Swedish Allsvenskan, 2022 season.** Event data from **Wyscout**, tracking data from **Signality** (25 Hz), synchronised and preprocessed by **Twelve Football AB**; the synchronisation was originally performed for Markou (2024). Raw scale: **419,684 event data points** and **9,409,375 synchronised freeze-frame tracking data points**. Standardised pitch 105 m × 68 m, origin bottom-left, all teams attacking left→right; defending-team coordinates mirrored. Unit of analysis = **possession chain** (Wyscout's pre-classified definition: possession continues while the same team retains control; terminated by ball out of play, goal, or the opponent gaining control through a meaningful action; brief/inconsequential opponent touches do not terminate it). Filtering: exclude possession_id = −1; **minimum 3 completed passes**; **maximum 60 s duration**. Filtering removed **53.7%** of chains, from **52,031 → 24,085**. Final datasets: 24,085 rows × 13 offensive features and 24,085 rows × 12 defensive features.

**Features / variables used — exhaustive.**

*Event-level offensive features (Table 7, 9 features):* Distance to Goal (Euclidean from event start to opponent goal centre); Vertical Progression (Δx); Horizontal Progression (Δy); Wing or Central Play (binary: wings = outer 14 m either side of the 68 m width); Attacking Team Area (convex-hull area over attacking outfield players, GK excluded); Attacking Team Speed (mean instantaneous speed m/s of attacking outfield players); Opponents in Front of Ball (count of defenders with x greater than ball x); Pressure within 6 Meters (linear-decay-weighted proximity score, radius 6 m, inspired by Andrienko et al. 2018); Pressure within 14 Meters (banded index — defenders <6 m weight 3, 6–10 m weight 2, 10–14 m weight 1; normalised against theoretical maximum 30 = 10 defenders × weight 3, giving 0–1; after Herold et al. 2022).

*Event-level defensive features (Table 8, 12 features):* Defensive Proximity (mean over attackers of distance to their nearest defender — stated as **novel**, "had not previously appeared in the literature and was developed in response to expert input highlighting the rise of man-to-man defensive strategies", p. 36); Numerical Superiority (defenders minus attackers within 15 m of ball); Defenders Proximity to Ball (mean distance of all defenders to ball); Closest Defender Proximity (min distance ball→defender); Pressure within 6 Meters; Pressure within 14 Meters; Defending Team Area (convex hull of outfield defenders); Vertical Compactness (max x − min x of outfield defenders); Horizontal Compactness (max y − min y); Defensive Line to Ball (x of deepest outfield defender minus ball x; negative = ball beyond last defender); Distance to Own Goal; Defenders Behind Ball.

*Possession-chain-level offensive features used for clustering (Table 9, 13 features), grouped by tactical category:*
- **General**: Total Offensive Actions (count of events of types pass, touch, acceleration, free_kick, shot, corner, offside, goal_kick, penalty, throw_in); Average Pass Length; Distance to Goal (mean).
- **Passing Patterns**: Vertical Switch (sum of Vertical Progression); Vertical Rotation (sum of |Vertical Progression|); Horizontal Rotation (sum of |Horizontal Progression|).
- **Passing Locations**: Total Central Plays; Total Wing Plays.
- **Team Movement**: Attacking Team Area (mean); Attacking Team Speed (mean).
- **Pressure**: Opponents in Front of Ball (mean); Pressure within 6 Meters (mean); Pressure within 14 Meters (mean).

*Possession-chain-level defensive features used for clustering (Table 10, 12 features):*
- **Marking/Zonal**: Defensive Proximity; Numerical Superiority; Defenders Proximity (Marking).
- **Pressure**: Closest Defender Proximity; Pressure within 6 Meters; Pressure within 14 Meters.
- **Compactness**: Defensive Team Area; Vertical Compactness; Horizontal Compactness.
- **Positioning**: Defensive Line to Ball; Defenders Behind Ball; Distance to Own Goal.

**Methodology.** Intermediate-theory research design (Edmondson & McManus, 2007) combining quantitative clustering with qualitative validation. Two-stage pipeline. *Stage 1*: possession-chain extraction → filtering/cleaning → event-level feature generation → aggregation to chain level → feature selection → scaling. Feature selection: **Pearson correlation** screening (flag |r| above ~0.9, keep the more interpretable of a correlated pair) plus **iterative evaluation** — multiple clustering runs across feature subsets judged on tactical interpretability, cluster distinctiveness, and stability across parameter settings. The offensive and defensive candidate pools were reduced **from around 40 initial candidate features to 13 (offense) and 12 (defence)**. Scaling: **z-score standardisation**, applied separately to offensive and defensive sets after selection. *Stage 2*: **K-Means** clustering (Euclidean, K-Means++ initialisation described in theory), run **independently for offence and defence**. k selection: **Elbow method + Silhouette score over k = 2 to 10**, combined with qualitative tactical assessment (explicitly following Gollan et al. 2018 and Cintia & Pappalardo 2021). Interpretation: mean **z-score heatmaps per cluster** (feature-in-cluster mean minus dataset mean, divided by dataset SD), plus **manual inspection of the possession chains closest to each cluster centroid** as archetypes (following Cintia & Pappalardo), plotted and animated over the pitch. Qualitative validation: repeated informal consultations with football data analysts, club-affiliated staff and the academic supervisor; plus **presentation to a seminar of over fifty participants** (practitioners, analysts and researchers); plus informal cross-checking of cluster interpretations against public/media narratives of how Allsvenskan teams played in 2022 (an approach adapted from Decroos et al. 2018).

**Evaluation metrics and values reported.** k = 6 chosen for both offence and defence. Offensive: elbow suggested possible elbows at k = 3 or k = 6; silhouette scores "all fairly equally low from cluster 5 and onwards, with a slight local minimum at 7 clusters". Defensive: elbow around 4–6; silhouette highest at k = 2, with k = 6 the highest among k ≥ 5. **The thesis is explicit that silhouette scores were "relatively low"** and argues at length that internal indices are the wrong criterion in this domain (Section 7.1.4). Reported downstream performance metrics per style (illustrative, IK Sirius 2022): e.g. Vertical Wing Attack 23.4% of chains, avg xT 0.22, total xG 10.76, 115 shots, 14 goals, 91.4% pass success, 12.2% goal conversion; Established Possession Circulation avg xT 0.33. Defensive (IK Sirius): Compact, Following Defence 25.9% of chains, avg xT against 0.27, xG against 10.07, 100 shots conceded, 12 goals conceded; High, Aggressive Defence 12.2% of chains, avg xT against 0.10, xG against 0.64, 12 shots, **0 goals conceded**.

**Key findings.** Six offensive styles: **Deep Low-Pressure Build-up; Direct Through-Play Attack; Counter Attack; Established Possession Circulation; Compact Midfield Build-up; Vertical Wing Attack.** Six defensive styles: **Wide, High, Passive Defence; Wide, Zonal, Passive Defence; Compact, Following Defence; Vertical Spread, Aggressive Marking; Compact, Deep Defence; High, Aggressive Defence.** Four applications developed in depth: (1) **Team DNA Profiling** (distribution of style shares per team, over arbitrary time horizons); (2) **Team Performance Analysis** (xT/xG/shots/goals/pass success/goal conversion per style); (3) **Opponent Scouting Analysis** — because each chain carries both an offensive label (attacker) and a defensive label (defender), styles can be **matched against each other**: "To our knowledge, no previous research has explored this kind of direct matching between offensive and defensive styles on a possession chain level" (p. 72); (4) **Player Scouting Analysis** — player xT decomposed by offensive style (top-10 midfielders by summed xT in Established Possession Circulation), plus a **transfer-impact model**: involvement rate per style estimated from frames-present-based playing-time proxy, then predicted total xT in a new club computed from the player's current per-style mean xT × the new team's number of chains in that style × involvement rate × expected playing time. Worked example: Jacob Bergström, Mjällby → Djurgården, predicted "similar but slightly higher total expected threat".

**Stated limitations (unusually candid and directly useful to us).**
- *Filtering*: 53.7% of chains removed; sequences containing key events such as goals may be missing. Mitigation suggested: retain filtered chains under labels ("too short", "too long", "missing data") so DNA profiles show where data is absent.
- *Features*: feature-selection subjectivity; removing correlated features may discard football-meaningful contextual distinctions; the **pressure index ignores player movement/speed** — "a player 7 meters away… but running rapidly towards the ball holder, could be considered more pressuring than an opponent a few meters away standing still"; **contextual bias by pitch zone** — what counts as "high pressure" differs between own-half build-up and the opponent's box; the **Vertical Switch** feature showed almost no variance and "may not be a meaningful or effective feature".
- *Method*: only a few clustering configurations explored due to the cost of qualitative validation; **outcome bias** — because chains differ in length/intent/success, clusters may partly encode outcome rather than intent ("a subtle spatial-success bias"); K-Means assumes spherical, similarly-sized clusters and enforces **hard assignment**, whereas "playing styles are often blended and fluid"; GMM (soft assignment) was considered and rejected for interpretability and overfitting reasons; **generalisability untested** beyond Allsvenskan 2022.
- *Validity of labels*: labelling is "inherently open to judgment"; no ground truth exists; low silhouette does not invalidate the clusters because "the quality of our cluster results are not determined by its silhouette score, but by its usefulness" (p. 79).

**Future work proposed.** Test generalisability across leagues/competitions/women's/youth; expand clustering configurations and add context-specific features (game state, pitch zone, opponent quality); **decompose possession chains into the "four phases of play" → 8 subphases** (Transition to attack / Build-Up / Midfield Progression / Chance Creation, and the four defensive mirrors) with subphase-specific feature sets, using a 10 s post-regain window for transitions and pitch-thirds for established phases; **train a supervised classifier on clustering-derived labels to enable near-real-time style assignment**; integrate more advanced analytics.

**Direct relevance to our project + what it justifies.** This is our **closest comparable thesis** and should be treated as the benchmark our work is positioned against.
- It gives us a fully specified, **implementable tracking-and-event feature set** (25 event-level + 25 chain-level features with formulas and rationale), with the citation trail for each feature (Andrienko et al. 2018 for pressure; Herold et al. 2022 for banded pressure; Forcher et al. 2024a for convex hull, numerical superiority, closest-defender pressing; Martín-Castellanos et al. 2024 for team length/width/defence height; Fernandez-Navarro et al. 2016 for wide vs narrow possession; Casey-Popovich 2024 for opponents behind ball).
- Cite it to justify **grouping features into named tactical categories** (General / Passing Patterns / Passing Locations / Team Movement / Pressure; Marking-Zonal / Pressure / Compactness / Positioning). This is a proto-ontology and the nearest published precedent for our metric→concept mapping.
- Cite it to justify **z-score heatmaps per cluster as the primary interpretation device** and **centroid-nearest exemplars as archetypes** — both directly reusable for presenting manager profiles.
- Cite it to justify **choosing k on interpretability rather than internal indices alone**, and to pre-empt the reviewer objection that our silhouettes are low: the thesis argues this position explicitly and at length, with Börgesson (2023) as supporting precedent.
- Cite it to justify **possession chains as the unit of analysis** if we go below match level, and to justify the **≥3 passes / ≤60 s filters** with their supporting citations (Forcher et al. 2024b; Ruano et al. 2015 for short sequences; Siegle & Lames 2012's finding that up to 38% of match time is lost to stoppages; Wang et al. 2022 for long-possession outliers).
- Its **player-scouting transfer model is the closest published thing to our player→manager compatibility estimate** — but it is style-*profile* fit to a *club*, computed by a deterministic arithmetic formula under strongly stated assumptions, not a manager-philosophy model and not explainable in tactical-concept terms. This is exactly where our contribution sits, and we must cite it honestly as prior art.
- Pitfalls to inherit deliberately: outcome bias; hard vs soft style assignment (argues for reporting *degrees* of fit rather than a single label — which suits a compatibility *score*); pitch-zone contextual bias in pressure features; and the warning that Vertical-Switch-type signed-sum features can be near-degenerate.

### 1.5 Tang, Wang & Zhang — Clustering football game situations via deep representation learning

**Full citation.** Tang Z, Wang X, Zhang S. *Clustering Football Game Situations via Deep Representation Learning.* Affiliations as printed: 1 Computer Science Department, Brown University; 2 Faculty of Physical Activity and Sports Sciences, Polytechnic University of Madrid; 3 Division of Sports Science and Physical Education, Tsinghua University. Footnote: "This work was completed when Zitian Tang was at Institute for Interdisciplinary Information Sciences, Tsinghua University." **Venue not stated in the PDF. Year not stated in the PDF. DOI not stated in the PDF.** Code released at https://github.com/ZitianTang/FootballSituation.

**Research question / objective.** "our goal is to cluster the freeze frames in StatsBomb 360 data into various situations and conduct performance analysis based on the discovered situations" (p. 1). Motivation: "a team can face different situations when they hold the ball at the same location" — so location-based aggregation of action values conflates tactically distinct contexts.

**Data source.** **StatsBomb 360 tactical event data** (freeze frames giving positions of all *visible* players when an event occurs). **English Premier League 2021/22** on-ball open-play events for model development; specifically the **90 games among ten selected teams** for building representation learning, clustering and Situational xT. Also applied to **EPL 2022/23** for cross-season comparison. Unit of analysis = **on-ball open-play event / freeze frame**. Note the acknowledged data property: player identities are unknown in freeze frames and the number of visible players varies.

**Features / variables used.** Pitch discretised to a **64 × 96 grid**; input to the encoder is a stack of **10 spatial feature maps**: (1) four **player-position maps** — event actor, offensive team players, defensive team players, goalkeepers (binary occupancy per cell); (2) three **relative-position-to-actor maps** — relative horizontal distance, relative vertical distance, straight-line distance; (3) three **relative-position-to-defending-goal maps** — relative horizontal, relative vertical, straight-line distance. Note: no ball-trajectory information.

Cluster-description attributes (Appendix, 11 named attributes): **Defensive distance** (on-ball player to closest opponent); **Team centroid depth** (x-coordinate of offensive team centroid, 0 = own goal to 120 = opponent goal); **Team centroid y-coordinate** (0 = left sideline to 80 = right sideline); **# Visible opponents**; **# Opponents in front** (larger x than on-ball player); **# Opponents in penalty area**; **# Visible teammates**; **# Teammates in front**; **# Teammates in penalty area**; **# Teammates in goal area**; **# Teammates in center space** (y ∈ [30, 50]).

**Methodology.** (1) **CNN-based auto-encoder** trained jointly on **three self-supervised/distilled pre-training tasks**: *soft Voronoi diagram construction* (ground truth V_{i,j} = exp(−O²/32) − exp(−D²/32) where O and D are distances to nearest offensive and defensive player; MSE loss), *pass success probability surface estimation* (surface first estimated for all frames by a **U-Net** trained on all pass events, then distilled into the auto-encoder with binary cross-entropy), and *next action prediction* (pass / carry / shot probabilities; a CNN teacher trained on all pass, carry and shot events, then MLP head with cross-entropy). Weighted joint loss **L = L_Voronoi + L_Pass + 0.1·L_Action**. Adam optimiser, learning rate 0.001, batch size 512, 200 epochs; checkpoint with smallest validation loss selected. (2) Pitch split into **30 zones**; freeze frames grouped by which zone the event actor is in; **K-Means with Euclidean distance run separately within each zone** in the representation space, "to better interpret the property of each resulting cluster". (3) **k chosen manually**: "we try different k (from 2 to 10) and manually find the one with the best interpretability for each zone". (4) Each cluster given a hand-written textual description from the computed attributes. (5) **Situational Expected Threat (Situational xT)** — a Markov model in which each *situation* (not each grid cell) is a state: xT_x = s_x·xG_x + p_x·Σ_z P_{x→z}·Σ_y T_{p,x→z,y}·xT_y + c_x·Σ_z C_{x→z}·Σ_y T_{c,x→z,y}·xT_y, with s+p+c = 1 and state 0 = failed action; parameters estimated from historical data and the linear system solved. Action value xT_add = 1{completed}·xT_y − xT_x for passes/carries, and 1{scores} − xT_x for shots.

**Evaluation metrics and values reported.** **95 situations discovered in total, two to four per zone.** Situational xT values are tabulated for all 95 situations, e.g. Zone 16 Situation 1 (in front of opponent's defensive line, defensive distance large) 0.87%; Zone 16-3 (between opponent's defensive lines) 0.61%; Zone 21-2 (defensive line retreated to penalty area, defensive distance small) 3.56%; Zone 27-1 (almost inside the goal area) 17.18%. Team-level metrics proposed: **Created occurrence / Created xT_add** (offence) and **Conceded occurrence / Conceded xT_add** (defence), both per game per situation. There is **no quantitative cluster-validity evaluation** (no silhouette, no ARI) — validation is entirely by attribute inspection and qualitative interpretability.

**Key findings.** Situational xT differs *within* the same zone: in midfield zones, xT is higher when defensive distance is large, "because the players tend to pass or carry the ball forward when the opponents are far away from them, while they prefer moving the ball backward under pressure" (p. 10). Team style is legible in **situation-occurrence distributions**: in Zones 15/16/17, Manchester City, Chelsea and Liverpool met the "in front of defensive line" situations far more than "between defensive lines", whereas Leeds United, Brentford and Everton more often had the ball between the opponent's defensive lines. Defensively, in Zones 10/11/12 Manchester City and Liverpool led opponents into small-defensive-distance or between-lines situations — "This illustrates their high-pressing defensive strategy" — while Tottenham, Manchester United and Everton preferred the large-defensive-distance situation, "align[ing] with their low-block defensive strategy" (pp. 13–14). Strength is situation-specific, not zone-specific: Man City created most xT_add in Situation 16-2 but negative value in 16-1 (bad carry choices at large defensive distance), while Tottenham was "completely the opposite". Cross-season case: Arsenal's created xT_add in Situation 10-2 rose from −0.0009 (2021/22) to 0.0039 (2022/23), attributed to long passes down the left flank by Gabriel Magalhães and Granit Xhaka; in Situation 13-3 from −0.0007 to 0.0057, from long carries down the right by Bukayo Saka and Ben White.

**Stated limitations.** "a crucial limitation of our grouping method is that a sample may not necessarily conform to the description of the situation it falls into. Any quantitative analysis derived from our results is only an approximation rather than accurate counting" (p. 6). Only visible players are captured; player identities are unknown; only on-ball open-play events; k chosen manually per zone.

**Direct relevance to our project + what it justifies.**
- Cite it to justify **conditioning player/team evaluation on tactical context rather than pitch location alone**. Its central argument — that identical locations conceal tactically different situations — transfers directly to our claim that identical raw metrics conceal tactically different roles.
- Cite it to justify **zone-wise / context-wise modelling to preserve interpretability**: it deliberately clusters *within* zones so that clusters are describable. That is the same design instinct as our knowledge layer.
- It provides a **published, quantified operationalisation of the two dimensions most central to Guardiola and Klopp**: pressing height / defensive line height (via defensive distance, opponents in front, and the "in front of / between defensive lines" taxonomy) and positional occupation (team centroid depth, teammates in centre space, teammates in penalty area). If we work with tracking or 360 data, these are ready-made concept definitions.
- Cite it as evidence that **Manchester City and Liverpool are empirically distinguishable from low-block teams by situation-occurrence profiles**, i.e. that manager philosophy leaves a measurable footprint at the situation level.
- Its **occurrence-vs-effectiveness distinction** ("a team may perform better in situations which they encounter fewer times") is a template we should copy for player–manager fit: separate *how often a player operates in the situations the manager's system generates* from *how well the player performs there*. That decomposition is exactly what a compatibility score needs.
- Caveats to inherit: manual k selection and no internal validity metrics — we should do better; and its own warning that cluster membership is approximate, which argues for probabilistic/soft fit scores.

### 1.6 Baattite (2023) — Machine learning-based football tactic and style analysis (capstone)

**Full citation.** Baattite A. *Machine Learning-Based Football Tactic and Style Analysis.* Capstone Design Final Report, Spring 2023. School of Science and Engineering, Al Akhawayn University. Supervised by Dr. Amine Abouaomar. **DOI not stated in PDF.**

**Research question / objective.** "to create a machine learning-based way of analyzing football match tactics and styles that overcomes the limitations of conventional analytic techniques… identify the most important playing style factors and develop models that forecast a team's playing style in a particular match as well as any potential counter-tactics" (pp. 2–3), plus deployment as a user-facing web application.

**Data source.** **StatsBomb open data**, accessed via the free API; raw scale reported as "about 6,000,000 samples and 183 features"; "a singe match my consist of over 3000 events". **2,622 matches** across UEFA Champions League, FA Women's Super League, FIFA World Cup, Indian Super League, La Liga, NWSL, Premier League, UEFA Euro, UEFA Women's Euro, Women's World Cup (Appendix B). After feature engineering: **38 features × 5,244 samples**. Unit of analysis = **team-half** — "we focused on analyzing each half of the matches separately and generated data and key performance indicators accordingly" (p. 17), on the rationale that the first half reflects preparation and the second reflects in-match coaching response. Data cleaning used the **StatsBombR** R package helpers: allclean(), cleanlocations(), goalkeeperinfo(), shotinfo(), freezeframeinfo() (including Density and Density in the cone), formatelapsedtime(), possessioninfo(). Standardisation via **StandardScaler**.

**Features / variables used — the 38 engineered KPIs (Table 5.2.1), with definitions as printed.**
- **P** Possession — total possession duration ÷ total match duration × 100.
- **PDT / PMT / PAT** Possession in defensive / middle / attacking third — x < 40 / 40 ≤ x ≤ 80 / x > 80.
- **PCA / PWA** Possession in central / wide areas — y between 20 and 60 / y not between 20 and 60.
- **PD** Pass Directness Score — average directness from pass length and angle.
- **FP / SP / BP** Forward / sideways / backward passes — by pass angle (forward 45–135°; sideways = not |angle| between 45 and 135; backward = negative angle between 45 and 135).
- **PDMT / PDAT** Passes from defensive third landing in middle third (start x ≤ 40, end x > 40) / attacking third (end x > 80).
- **C / EC** Crosses (% of attacking sequences ending with a cross) / Early Crosses (crosses from start location x < 100).
- **S / SOS / SIB / SOB** Shots (% of attacking sequences ending with a shot) / Shoot On Sight (shots taken as soon as the player is not under pressure, as % of total shots) / Shots inside box (location > 100 and y between 20 and 40) / Shots outside box (location not greater than 100).
- **RDT / RMT / RAT** Ball recovery in defensive / middle / attacking third — % of defensive actions in that third over total defensive actions.
- **RCA / RWA** Ball recovery in central / wide areas — same, by area.
- **T** Tempo — directness score of passes × time duration of the pass (ball-movement duration).
- **WBIB** Work Ball Into Box — % of attacking sequences ending neither with a cross nor a shot, and attacking actions in the attacking third but not in the penalty box.
- **DrL** Dribble Less — count of dribbles by the team.
- **RAD** Run At Defense — ball carries starting in the attacking third continuing more than 6 m forward.
- **EA** Expressive Actions — acrobatic/artistic actions (nutmeg, Panenka penalty, etc.).
- **PI** Pressing Intensity — **PPDA**: passes made by the attacking team ÷ total defensive actions by the defending team in the pitch area x > 40.
- **CP / HS** Counter-press (pressing actions within 5 seconds of an open-play turnover) / Hold Shape (the opposite of counter-pressing).
- **TP** Through pass — % of attacking sequences ending with a through pass (pass cutting the last line of defence).
- **CB** Cutbacks — % of attacking sequences ending with a cut-back pass (low/ground pass originating in the crossing zone, ending in the penalty box).
- **BMD** Be More Disciplined — rate of disciplined over undisciplined/aggressive defensive actions.
- **PFSP** Play For Set Pieces — set-piece efficiency percentage.
- **TM** Team Mentality — rate of attacking over defensive actions.
- **CA** Counter Attack — attacking sequences originating from a counter-attack.
Additional abbreviations listed in the front matter but not all defined in Table 5.2.1: PIS (Pass Into Space), POD (Play Out Of Defense), BME (Be More Expressive), UOT (Use Offside Trap), LOE (Line of Engagement), DL (Defensive Line), DW (Defensive Width), UTM (Use Tighter Marking), HS (Hold Shape).

**Methodology.** **Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA)** with **principal axis factoring** extraction. Suitability checks: **KMO = 0.765** (also reported as 0.76) and **Bartlett's test of sphericity p < 0.001**. Communalities computed; variables with communality **< 0.5 dropped**. Factor retention by the **Kaiser criterion (eigenvalue ≥ 1)** → **9 factors**; supported by a scree plot. **Varimax rotation**. Factor scores computed per team by the **regression method**. Downstream supervised learning on engineered features = **per-match difference between the two teams' factor scores**, plus goals scored and conceded. **22 classification models** evaluated (Logistic Regression, Ridge, Passive Aggressive, Perceptron, SGD, KNN, Decision Tree, Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, AdaBoost, Extra Trees, Bagging, SVC, LinearSVC, NuSVC, GaussianNB, MultinomialNB, BernoulliNB, MLP, LDA, QDA, GaussianProcess, XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost as listed) and **22 regression models**. Deployment: **Streamlit** web app where the user selects team, competition, season and opponent, and receives predicted playing styles, suggested counter-tactics, and KPI visualisations (expected threat locations, passing network, heat maps, pass flow, passing zones, shots and xG).

**Evaluation metrics and values reported.** EFA: eigenvalues 10.62, 2.44, 2.40, 1.74, 1.66, 1.52, 1.37, 1.27, 1.23; proportion of variance 0.35, 0.08, 0.07, 0.05, 0.05, 0.04, 0.04, 0.04, 0.03; **cumulative variance 0.76 (≈76%)**. Full rotated loading matrix printed for 29 retained variables. Classification: **LDA selected — accuracy 0.73 train / 0.75 test; precision 0.76 / 0.75; recall 0.89 / 0.93; F1 0.82 / 0.83.** Overfitting explicitly identified in Decision Tree (1.00 train / 0.61 test), Random Forest (1.00 / 0.71), Extra Trees (1.00 / 0.71), Bagging (0.99 / 0.70). Regression: **PCR selected — MSE 2.03, R² 0.23, MAE 1.07, Explained Variance 0.23, Max Error 6.55, Median Absolute Error 0.83** (identical to Linear Regression and Ridge on these figures). Worst: Decision Tree R² −0.60; Gaussian Process R² −0.83. MAPE judged unreliable "due to the presence of very small or zero values in the target variable".

**Key findings.** Nine bipolar factors interpreted as playing styles (Table 5.3.4.5), each with a named opposite pole:
1. Possession and Tempo ↔ Direct and Slow Build-up
2. Balanced Build-up ↔ Narrow and High-Press Play
3. Dribbling and Expressiveness ↔ Structured and Collective Play
4. Defensive Recovery ↔ High-Risk Attacking Play
5. Central vs. Wide Area Regains ↔ Balanced Regains
6. Attacking Intent ↔ Defensive and Conservative Play
7. Crossing and Working Ball into the Box ↔ Central Build-up and Short Passing Play
8. Long Shot Threat ↔ Penetrative and Close-Range Finishing
9. High-Press Attacking and Ball Recovery ↔ Low-Press Defensive Play and Direct Attacking
Loading examples: Tempo loads 0.88 on Factor 1, Forward Passes −0.86, Sideways Passes 0.80, Pass Directness −0.69; PWA 0.85 and PDT 0.79 on Factor 2; BME 0.93 and DrL 0.92 on Factor 3; RDT −0.88, RMT 0.75, PI −0.56 on Factor 4; RCA −0.96 / RWA 0.96 on Factor 5; S 0.87 and SIB 0.91 on Factor 6; EC 0.98 and WBIB 0.60 on Factor 7; SOB 1.00 on Factor 8; RAT 0.54 and PAT 0.48 on Factor 9.

**Stated limitations.** The report does **not** contain a dedicated limitations section. Weaknesses acknowledged in passing: overfitting of tree ensembles; MAPE unreliability; the literature review notes that a prior commercial approach (Ruiz, 2016) "falls short in providing in-depth information on the process and does not offer an explanation for the selection of the predefined playing styles". Rigour concerns visible to the reader and worth flagging in our own review: no cross-validation scheme reported for the classifiers; the outcome variable is described inconsistently ("either win or no win" used for both the classification *and* the goal-difference regression); the factor-loading table contains values of 1.00 and −0.96/0.96 pairs (RCA/RWA are near-perfect complements, so Factor 5 is arguably an artefact of a compositional constraint); and several front-matter abbreviations (PIS, POD, LOE, DL, DW, UTM, UOT) never appear in the KPI table.

**Direct relevance to our project + what it justifies.**
- Its **38-KPI catalogue with explicit computational definitions from StatsBomb event data is the most operationally reusable feature dictionary in the corpus** for an event-data-only project. If we cannot obtain tracking data, this is the feature layer we can actually build, and every metric comes with a stated formula (thresholds in StatsBomb's 120×80 coordinates).
- Cite it to justify **EFA with principal axis factoring, KMO/Bartlett suitability checks, communality ≥ 0.5 screening, Kaiser eigenvalue ≥ 1 retention, scree-plot corroboration, Varimax rotation, and regression-method factor scores** — a complete, citable factor-analysis protocol.
- Cite it to justify **bipolar interpretation of factors** (each factor read as a style and its opposite) — the same device Plakias et al. use, and a natural way to express a manager's position on each tactical dimension.
- Cite it to justify **team-half rather than whole-match aggregation** if we want to detect in-match tactical adjustment.
- Cite it as precedent for **deploying the framework as an interactive artefact** (Streamlit) and for producing **counter-tactic recommendations** from style predictions.
- Use it as a **cautionary exemplar** for our Evaluation chapter: it demonstrates how weak style-based match-outcome prediction is (R² = 0.23, LDA accuracy 0.75) and how badly tree ensembles overfit small style-score datasets. It is also a good argument for why our project should *not* be evaluated primarily on outcome prediction.

### 1.7 Wang, Veličković, Hennes et al. (2023) — TacticAI

**Full citation.** Wang Z, Veličković P, Hennes D, Tomašev N, Prince L, Kaisers M, Bachrach Y, Elie R, Wenliang LK, Piccinini F, Spearman W, Graham I, Connor J, Yang Y, Recasens A, Khan M, Beauguerlange N, Sprechmann P, Moreno P, Heess N, Bowling M, Hassabis D, Tuyls K. *TacticAI: an AI assistant for football tactics.* arXiv:2310.10553v2 [cs.LG], 17 Oct 2023 (document dated 2023-10-18). Affiliations: Google DeepMind; Liverpool FC (W. Spearman); University of Alberta (M. Bowling); I. Graham "Work completed while at Liverpool FC". © 2023 Google DeepMind. **No journal DOI stated in this PDF.**

**Research question / objective.** To build and validate an AI assistant for corner-kick tactics with both predictive and generative components, addressing three benchmark tasks: **receiver prediction, shot prediction, and tactic recommendation via guided generation**. Corners were chosen because they occur frequently (~10 per match, citing Shaw & Gopaladesikan), are taken from a fixed position, and offer an immediate scoring opportunity.

**Data source.** **9,693 corner kicks from the 2020–2021 Premier League seasons**, provided by Liverpool FC, from four merged sources: (i) **tracking data** at 25 fps for all players and the ball, with velocities derived from positions by filtering — only the frame at which the kick is taken is used; (ii) **event stream data** (passes, shots, goals); (iii) **line-up data** (heights, weights, roles); (iv) **game data** (game day, stadium, pitch length and width in metres). **2,517 corners filtered out** for failed alignment (missing tracking frames or event labels), leaving **7,176 valid corners**, split **80% train / 20% test** with a consistent split across all three tasks. Label distribution for shot prediction: **1,736 shot / 5,440 no-shot** (positive-to-negative ratio 0.21). Unit of analysis = **one corner kick, represented as a fully connected 22-node graph**. Data are not publicly available due to licensing restrictions.

**Features / variables used (Tables 1 and 2 — deliberately minimal).**
- **Node features (per player):** XY positions; XY velocities; player weights (kg); player heights (cm); ball possession (binary indicator of whether this player possesses the ball).
- **Edge features (all 22×22 pairs, graph fully connected):** teammate-or-opponent one-hot encoding.
- **Global features:** Receiver ID (one-hot; used for shot prediction and guided generation); Shot indicator (binary; used for guided generation).
- Explicitly *excluded*: ball movement (neither position nor velocity), and explicit player-to-player distances. Players are otherwise anonymous. Preprocessing: heights/weights downscaled by 100; missing profiles defaulted to 180 cm / 75 kg (385 such occurrences out of 213,246 = 22 × 9,693); positions zero-centred and normalised onto a 10 m × 10 m pitch with velocities rescaled accordingly; default pitch 110 m × 63 m when dimensions were missing.

**Methodology.** Geometric deep learning over graphs. Core architecture: **four GATv2 group-convolution layers**, 8 attention heads each, 4 latent features per player, giving H ∈ ℝ^{4×22×4}. **D₂ dihedral symmetry group** {id, ↔, ↕, ↔↕} is exploited to encode quadrant symmetries of the pitch: all four reflected "views" are generated and allowed to interact through **group convolution** (Cohen & Welling), H_g^{(t)} = (1/|𝔊|)Σ_𝔥 g_G(H_𝔥^{(t−1)} ‖ H_{g⁻¹𝔥}^{(t−1)}). For the two **invariant** tasks (receiver, shot) the four views are frame-averaged; for the **equivariant** task (position/velocity adjustment) only the identity view is used. Decoders: node-wise linear classifier + softmax cross-entropy (receiver); mean-pooled graph representation + sigmoid binary cross-entropy (shot); **VAE** head per player mapping to a 2-D Gaussian N(μ_u, σ_u) with the reparameterisation trick and an outcome-conditional VAE loss L(θ,φ) = −E[log p_φ(x_u|h_u,o)] + KL(q_θ(h_u|X,o) ‖ p(h_u|o)) (guided generation). Shot probability is **decomposed conditionally**: ℙ(shot) = Σ_i ℙ(shot|receiver=i)·ℙ(receiver=i), with ground-truth receiver fed at training and all receivers marginalised at inference. Training: Adam, L2 weight decay, NVIDIA Tesla P100 GPUs, 50,000 steps for the ablation budget. Hyperparameters (S.Table 1): batch size 256/128/128, learning rate 1e-4/1e-4/5e-5, L2 1e-4/0/1e-4, β₁ 0.9, β₂ 0.999, ε 1e-8, graph attention layers 4/2/2, random seed 42 for receiver/shot/guided-generation respectively.

**Evaluation metrics and values reported.**
- *Receiver prediction* (top-3 accuracy), ablation S.Table 2: **CNN 0.364 ± 0.031; Deep Sets 0.713 ± 0.022; MPNN 0.723 ± 0.017; GATv2 0.748 ± 0.021; GATv2 + D₂ frame averaging 0.780 ± 0.011; GATv2 + D₂ group convolution 0.782 ± 0.039** (best).
- *Shot prediction* (F1, chosen because the data are imbalanced), S.Table 3: **GATv2 unconditional 0.521 ± 0.027; + receiver conditional 0.677 ± 0.036; + receiver conditional + D₂ group convolution 0.712 ± 0.011.** (The main text reports the two-phase approach as reaching **0.64 ± 0.02** and the unconditional as 0.52 ± 0.03 — the main text and supplementary table differ, and both figures appear in the PDF.)
- *Generated adjustments vs real corners*: an MLP classifier could not distinguish them, **F1 = 0.53 ± 0.05** (chance level).
- *Saliency of adjustments*: over 100 corners with threatening shots, defensive refinements reduced mean predicted shot probability from **0.75 ± 0.14 to 0.69 ± 0.16 (z = 2.62, p < 0.001)**; attacking refinements raised it from **0.18 ± 0.16 to 0.31 ± 0.26 (z = −4.46, p < 0.001)**.
- *Latent space*: t-SNE of latent team embeddings for 1,024 corners shows in-swing vs out-swing and attacking vs defending separation; **"no clear clusters are observed in the raw input space"** (S.Figure 1).

**Expert case-study design and results — described precisely, because it is our template.** Five experts affiliated with Liverpool FC: **three data scientists, one video analyst, one coaching assistant**. All task details were withheld from raters beforehand; team and player identities were never revealed — only positions, velocities, and attacking/defending team membership.
- **Task 1 — realism.** 50 held-out Premier League corners; for a subset, TacticAI's generative head replaced the real setup with an adjustment (minimising or maximising shot probability). Raters classified each as real or generated. Mean rater F1 = **0.60 ± 0.04** (individual 0.54, 0.64, 0.65, 0.62, 0.56). Scoring each *sample* +1 if judged real and averaging across raters, there was **no significant difference between real and generated corners (z = 0.34, p > 0.05)**. The paper documents the three heuristics raters used (spotting something markedly unusual; judging plausibility regardless of tactical quality; memory-based retrieval).
- **Task 2 — receiver plausibility.** On the same 50 corners, raters named the attacking players most likely to make first contact; free choice of how many. Scored +1 if at least one rater-named receiver appeared in TacticAI's top-3. Mean top-3 accuracy across raters **0.79 ± 0.18** (0.82 ± 0.17 real, 0.77 ± 0.21 generated), with **no significant difference between real and generated (z = 0.968, p > 0.05)**. One-way **ANOVA + Tukey's test** on rater agreement: raters differed significantly (**F₁,₄ = 14.46, p < 0.001**) forming **three clusters** (A≈E, z = 1.41 p > 0.05; B≈D, z = 2.53 p > 0.05; C distinct) — i.e. deliberate measurement of *inter-rater heterogeneity* as a finding, not a nuisance.
- **Task 3 — retrieval.** 50 reference corners, each paired with one retrieved corner: nearest neighbour either in **TacticAI's graph-level latent space** or, as baseline, by **cosine similarity of raw features**. Raters scored +1 if the pair was "usefully similar". Recall: **TacticAI 0.63 ± 0.09 vs baseline 0.33 ± 0.10**; averaged per pair, TacticAI's ratings significantly higher (**z = 2.34, p < 0.05**); high inter-rater agreement for TacticAI retrievals (**F₁,₄ = 1.01, p > 0.1**). Salient features raters used are enumerated: in- vs out-swinging, short-corner option, zonal vs man-marking, number of players in the 18-yard box, goalkeeper positioning, and whether the corners likely shared an attacking/defending team or game. Raters noted top-1 retrieval is too strict — in practice an analyst would review 10–20 retrievals.
- **Task 4 — usefulness of adjustments.** 50 held-out corners; TacticAI adjusted only the **defending** team's positions and velocities to reduce predicted shot probability (chosen to match the real-world workload: the attacking team dictates the setup, the defence responds). Reference and adjusted versions shown side by side, **with the order reversed for a random subset to control for bias**. Raters scored each adjustment **+1 salient improvement / 0 no salient difference / −1 salient worsening**. Mean of per-rater averages **0.7 ± 0.1**; **45 of 50 situations (90%)** judged favourable on average by majority voting. One-sample t-tests: pooled **t₄₉^avg = 9.20, p < 0.001**; each rater individually significant (t₄₉ = 5.84, 7.30, 7.00, 6.04, 7.88; all p < 0.001); high inter-rater agreement (**F₁,₄ = 0.45, p > 0.05**). Two adjustments were judged only "possibly useful" contingent on player physical profile — out of scope since the model only had height and weight.

**Key findings.** Geometric deep learning delivers data efficiency under scarce gold-standard data; graph structure and reflection symmetry both measurably help; conditioning shot prediction on the predicted receiver is a large win; the latent space supports retrieval that experts prefer 2:1 over a raw-feature baseline; and generative adjustments are both indistinguishable from real tactics and favoured 90% of the time.

**Stated limitations.** Requires position and velocity estimates for all players at execution time, derived from high-quality tracking data whose availability from providers is "limited to top leagues"; broadcast-video tracking would broaden reach but add noise; "our method does not explicitly model exogenous (aleatoric) uncertainty, which would be valuable context for the football analyst"; GAT attention *would* allow introspection of salient factors but this was not performed; only one team is adjusted at a time (both teams react simultaneously in reality); player features limited to height and weight. Future work: generalisation to other set pieces and other team sports with suspended play, and "integrate a natural language interface that enables domain-grounded conversations with the assistant".

**Direct relevance to our project + what it justifies.**
- **The expert-validation design is the single most valuable transferable artefact in the corpus for us.** Our framework produces manager profiles and compatibility scores for which *no ground truth exists*. TacticAI shows exactly how to make that defensible: pre-registered blinded tasks, a small expert panel with heterogeneous roles, per-item scoring on a simple {−1, 0, +1} or binary scale, averaging per item *and* per rater, order randomisation to control bias, one-sample t-tests against zero, ANOVA/Tukey for inter-rater structure, and a comparison against a **naive baseline** (raw-feature cosine similarity). We should adapt Tasks 3 and 4 almost directly: *Task 3 analogue* — given a player, is the manager our model ranks as most compatible "usefully similar" in philosophy to the player's profile, versus a raw-metric-similarity baseline? *Task 4 analogue* — given a player–manager pair, do experts judge our explanation as a salient, correct account of the fit (+1/0/−1)?
- Cite it to justify **beating a raw-feature baseline as the core validity claim of a learned representation** — and note its t-SNE finding that structure visible in the latent space is absent in raw feature space. This is precisely the argument we need for the Football Knowledge Layer: mapping metrics into tactical concepts should reveal structure that raw metrics do not.
- Cite it to justify **F1 (not accuracy) under class imbalance**, and **ablation tables that isolate each design decision's contribution**.
- Cite it to justify **encoding domain structure into the model architecture rather than learning it from data** when data are scarce — the geometric-deep-learning argument ("high-quality tracking data is often limited—with only a few hundred matches played each year in every league"). Our knowledge layer is the same move in a different guise: injecting football priors to buy data efficiency and interpretability.
- Honest counterpoint we must acknowledge: TacticAI states it "incorporates minimal domain knowledge, and does not rely on intricate feature engineering". It is the corpus's strongest example of a *knowledge-light* system succeeding. Our claim must therefore be about **explainability in tactical language and manager-level abstraction**, not about knowledge-informed ML being universally superior.
- Also note the explicit unmet need it names — a natural-language interface for domain-grounded tactical conversation — which our explanation layer partially addresses.

### 2.3 Normalisation practices — what the literature actually does

**What is universal:**
- **Z-score standardisation before distance-based clustering** is the near-unanimous practice: Plakias et al. 2025 ("standardized using z-scores… performed by variable"), Zhong et al. ("Z-score standardization was applied to normalize feature scales"), Bolin & Vesterlind (z-score, applied separately to offensive and defensive sets after feature selection), Baattite (StandardScaler), and in Gu's review De França, Gollan and Branquinho explicitly, with Moffatt and He reporting "Yes" without detail. Gu et al. note that preprocessing was **not reported at all** in Clavijo and Yi.
- **Z-scores are also the standard interpretation device** for clusters: Bolin & Vesterlind compute (cluster mean − dataset mean)/dataset SD per feature and display heatmaps, citing Nguyen et al. (2023), Gollan et al. (2018) and Gyarmati et al. (2014) for the practice.
- **Rate/percentage construction is the dominant way to control for volume** in event-data work. Baattite defines most KPIs as percentages: possession as duration ÷ match duration × 100; crosses, shots, through passes, cut-backs as *% of attacking sequences* ending that way; ball recoveries as *% of total defensive actions* in that third or area; Shoot-On-Sight as % of total shots. Plakias et al. 2025 aggregate by taking the **mean of each variable per team across its matches** (5,998 observations → 174 team means). Zhong et al. use **per-match team values**.

**What the literature does NOT do — state this honestly:**
- **Per-90 normalisation is never mentioned in any of the seven papers.** Not once.
- **Possession-adjustment and per-100-passes normalisation are never mentioned.** Zhong et al. *diagnose* the need for it (they show possession confounds every volume metric, and their two clusters differ by 15.5 possession points) but they do not implement it.
- **Percentile normalisation within position is never mentioned.** No paper computes position-relative percentiles.
- Where non-z-score scaling does occur it is idiosyncratic: TacticAI zero-centres and normalises player coordinates to a **10 m × 10 m** pitch with velocities rescaled accordingly, and divides heights and weights by 100 — i.e. scaling for numerical conditioning, not for exposure. Bolin & Vesterlind's 14 m pressure index is normalised against a **theoretical maximum of 30** to give a 0–1 index — a nice example of principled bounded normalisation. Their player **involvement rate** normalises a player's event count by team chains in that style and by an estimated playing-time fraction (frames-present-based, with an added constant 3 percentage points "since this seemed to improve the estimate") — the corpus's only exposure adjustment, and an admittedly crude one.

**What the literature recommends:** Gu et al. recommend standardised reporting frameworks rather than any specific normalisation, pointing to the **Rojas-Valverde et al. (2021) 21-item PCA checklist** (which "emphasiz[es] detailed reporting of variable selection, data suitability assessments, extraction and rotation methods, and result interpretation") and Golalipour et al. (2022) for clustering. Bolin & Vesterlind add a warning our project must heed: features carry **contextual bias by pitch zone** — "the practical interpretation of what constitutes 'high pressure' could differ between a team's build-up phase near their own goal and an attacking sequence close to the opponent's penalty area" — implying that zone- or phase-conditional normalisation is needed. They also flag **outcome bias** as a normalisation-adjacent problem: unequal chain lengths mean clusters may encode success rather than intent.

**Implication for us.** There is an open, citable methodological gap here: **the playing-style literature has no established exposure-normalisation standard.** If we normalise player metrics per-90 *and* possession-adjusted *and* as percentiles within position, we are going beyond the corpus and must justify each choice from adjacent literature or from first principles, while citing Zhong et al. for the confounding problem and Bolin & Vesterlind for the zone-context problem.

### 2.4 Dimensionality reduction — consensus and thresholds

| Approach | Papers | Details |
|---|---|---|
| **PCA / factor analysis (linear)** | Gu review: **11 of 25 studies (44%)** — the modal method; Baattite (EFA, principal axis factoring); Plakias et al. 2025 (re-uses 19 factors from Plakias et al. 2023) | The consensus default |
| **t-SNE** | Gu review: 1 study alone + used in combination in others (3 studies total described); TacticAI (for latent-space visualisation only) | Used for **visualisation and similarity mapping, not for feature construction**. Gu et al. caution: "its sensitivity to parameter tuning and high computational demands necessitate meticulous implementation to maintain interpretability" |
| **UMAP** | Appears only as a search keyword in Gu et al.'s Table 1 | **No paper in this corpus applies UMAP.** Do not claim UMAP is standard practice. |
| **Autoencoder (nonlinear)** | Zhong et al. (30 vars → 2-D latent, then k-means) | Justified explicitly against PCA: "PCA is a linear method and often fails to capture nonlinear relationships, limiting its effectiveness in identifying playing styles" |
| **Deep representation learning (self-supervised, multi-task)** | Tang et al. (CNN auto-encoder, 3 pre-training tasks) | For spatial freeze frames where a distance metric is otherwise undefinable |
| **Geometric deep learning / GNN embeddings** | TacticAI (GATv2 + D₂ group convolution) | Latent player/graph representations; t-SNE used only to *inspect* them |

**Thresholds and retention rules actually reported:**
- **Suitability**: KMO minimum thresholds across the Gu corpus span **0.5, 0.53, 0.6, 0.68, 0.69, 0.7, 0.76, 0.77, 0.8, 0.9**; Baattite reports **KMO = 0.765** and cites >0.6 as acceptable. Bartlett's test of sphericity reported in **7 of 11** PCA studies (Gu) and by Baattite (p < 0.001).
- **Retention**: **Kaiser criterion (eigenvalue > 1)** in the large majority (Gu's Table 4 shows "Factor: >1" for most studies; Baattite uses eigenvalue ≥ 1 giving 9 factors). **Wedding et al. used eigenvalue > 1.2.** **Scree plots** used by Baattite as corroboration. **Parallel analysis is not used or mentioned in any of the seven papers** — do not claim it as standard.
- **Loadings**: cut-off **>0.6** in most studies (Gu's Table 4); **>0.7** in Fernandez-Navarro; not reported in Martín-Castellanos, Castellano, Lopez-Valenciano.
- **Rotation**: **Varimax** in Ruan (×2), Gómez, Fernandez-Navarro, Martín-Castellanos, Castellano, Plakias et al.42, Zhou, Lago-Peñas; not reported in Wedding (×2), Lopez-Valenciano, Kong.
- **Variance-explained achieved** (not a threshold, but the empirical range): **51.0% – 87.5%**, median around 79%. Solutions retaining 2 factors reach 77–80%; solutions retaining 7–9 factors reach 74–85%; the 19-factor solution reaches 84.8%. **No paper states a minimum variance-explained threshold as a decision rule** — variance explained is reported, not used to select.

**Consensus statement for our report.** PCA/factor analysis with Varimax rotation, Kaiser retention, loading cut-off ≥0.6, and KMO + Bartlett suitability reporting is the defensible default, endorsed by a 25-study systematic review, and preferred precisely because the resulting factors are interpretable as football concepts. Nonlinear alternatives (autoencoders, self-supervised CNNs, GNNs) demonstrably capture more structure but at a documented cost in interpretability — which is why Bolin & Vesterlind chose K-Means over GMM and why Plakias et al. 2025 criticise t-SNE-based approaches for lacking "soccer-specific interpretability".

### 2.5 Clustering — algorithms, choosing k, validity, stability

| Algorithm | Papers |
|---|---|
| **k-means** | Gu review: the dominant algorithm (Plakias ×2, De França, Gollan, Moffatt, Branquinho, Yi, Kong, da Silva); Zhong (on autoencoder latents); Bolin & Vesterlind; Tang (per zone) |
| **Hierarchical** | Plakias et al. 2025 (**Ward's method + squared Euclidean distance**); Gu review: Diquigiovanni (Louvain and UPGMA), Clavijo (Ward) |
| **GMM (soft assignment)** | Gu review: He et al. (k chosen by **AIC & BIC**); considered and explicitly rejected by Bolin & Vesterlind |
| **Two-step clustering** | Gu review: Wedding et al.8 — BIC-based automatic clustering then **silhouette** validation |

**How k is chosen:**
- **Silhouette maximisation**: Zhong et al. (highest silhouette → k = 2); Wedding et al. as a validation step.
- **Elbow method**: Moffatt et al.; Bolin & Vesterlind.
- **Elbow + silhouette + qualitative tactical judgement**: Bolin & Vesterlind (k = 6 for both offence and defence, over the range k = 2–10), explicitly following Gollan et al. and Cintia & Pappalardo.
- **AIC / BIC**: He et al. (GMM).
- **Dendrogram inspection with staged re-clustering**: Plakias et al. 2025.
- **Manual interpretability sweep**: Tang et al. ("we try different k (from 2 to 10) and manually find the one with the best interpretability for each zone").
- **Not chosen / not reported**: Gu et al.'s central methodological finding is that **only 2 of 9 clustering studies (22%) explicitly described the criterion for selecting the optimal number of clusters.**

**Internal validity indices used:** silhouette (Zhong, Wedding, Bolin & Vesterlind); ARI (Diquigiovanni); AIC/BIC (He); Coefficient of Unalikeability (He); elbow/WSS (Moffatt, Bolin & Vesterlind). **Davies–Bouldin and Calinski–Harabasz are never used or mentioned in any of the seven papers.** Do not present them as established practice in this literature; if we use them, we introduce them.

**External / discriminative validation:** Plakias et al. 2025 is the strongest example — **discriminant analysis with Wilks' λ per variable plus overall correct-classification rates of 90.8% / 100% / 97.2%**, followed by t-tests with Cohen's d, and a rule that only |d| > 0.8 differences define a style. Wedding et al. similarly used discriminant analysis (81% win–loss classification). This "cluster then discriminate" pattern is a far more persuasive validity argument than silhouette and we should adopt it.

**Stability:** essentially unaddressed. Bolin & Vesterlind assessed "the stability of the clusters across different parameter settings" informally within their iterative feature-selection loop, and Zhong et al. used a fixed random seed and a single 70/30 split for the ablation. **No paper in the corpus performs bootstrap resampling, consensus clustering, or a formal cluster-stability index.** Gu et al. cite Golalipour et al. (2022) as recommending assessment of "robustness" but the reviewed studies do not do it. **This is a cheap, genuinely novel-in-context rigour win for our project.**

**The silhouette debate — important for us.** Bolin & Vesterlind devote a full section (7.1.4) to arguing that low silhouette scores do not invalidate football style clusters: possession chains "shared common structural and spatial elements… These similarities, while tactically meaningful in different contexts, can blur distinctions in the abstract feature space"; "football is not a geometrically neat domain"; "the quality of our cluster results are not determined by its silhouette score, but by its usefulness" (p. 79), with Börgesson (2023) cited as precedent. Zhong et al. take the opposite position, selecting k purely on silhouette and arriving at k = 2. Our report should acknowledge this as an unresolved methodological tension and take an explicit, justified position.

**Class imbalance handling.** Two explicit strategies, both worth citing. (1) **Collapse the rare class**: Zhong et al. recode outcome as binary WIN vs NOWIN because "draws are difficult to predict due to their stochastic nature and low frequency… This introduces class imbalance and reduces model stability" (p. 577), citing Menardi & Torelli (2012). (2) **Choose an imbalance-robust metric**: TacticAI uses F1 rather than accuracy, footnoting that "it is commonly used in binary classification problems over imbalanced datasets" and stating the **positive-to-negative ratio of 0.21**. No paper in the corpus uses SMOTE, class weighting, or resampling.

**Small-sample handling.** TacticAI is the reference: it addresses scarcity architecturally, via **symmetry-based data efficiency** ("high-quality tracking data is often limited—with only a few hundred matches played each year in every league"), plus **L2 regularisation** on every model. Zhong et al. use LASSO regularisation and multi-method feature-selection intersection to keep the predictor set parsimonious. Baattite demonstrates the failure mode of not doing this: perfect training scores and ~0.61–0.71 test scores for Decision Tree, Random Forest, Extra Trees and Bagging. Gu et al. note the underlying reality — the review's sample sizes range from 8 to 2,996 matches, and studies with 59–64 matches (World Cup analyses) are common.

### 2.7 Explainability / XAI

| Mechanism | Papers | How model output is translated into domain language |
|---|---|---|
| **SHAP** | Zhong et al. (SHAP on the selected SVM/KNN per style, citing Lundberg & Lee 2017); also cites Moustakidis et al. 2023 as prior SHAP-in-football work | SHAP summary and dependence plots ranked by influence; the paper then narrates each top feature as a tactical statement ("excessive possession combined with frequent key passes may indicate inefficiency in breaking down compact defenses") |
| **Feature-importance / selection as explanation** | Zhong et al. (Boruta importance, XGBoost importance, LASSO coefficients, intersected) | Retained KPI lists per style presented as the tactical signature of that style |
| **Factor loadings as explanation** | Baattite (rotated loading matrix → named bipolar styles); Plakias et al. 2025 (19 latent variables with named high/low tactical behaviours) | "the highest the factor loading value of a KPI, the more that KPI is representing that playing style, for example The tiki-taka playing style would have high values in the possession and low values in the passing directness" (Baattite, p. 20) |
| **Z-score heatmaps + centroid archetypes** | Bolin & Vesterlind; Gollan et al. and Gyarmati et al. as cited precedent | Per-cluster feature z-scores read off a heatmap, then the possession chains nearest the centroid are plotted and animated on the pitch and described in words |

**Assessment.** Only **one** of the seven papers (Zhong et al. 2026) applies a modern model-agnostic XAI method (SHAP). Permutation importance is used by none. The dominant "explainability" in this literature is **structural interpretability by construction** — factor loadings, z-score profiles, named bipolar dimensions, and hand-written cluster descriptions. This is good news for our framework: a knowledge layer that maps metrics → concepts → dimensions is *the* field-consistent explainability mechanism, and SHAP over knowledge-layer features (rather than raw metrics) is a defensible, minimally novel-but-real methodological contribution. Note also the future-work reference we should chase: **Rahimian, Flisar & Sumpter (2025), "Automated Explanation of Machine Learning Models of Footballing Actions in Words", arXiv:2504.00767** (cited in Bolin & Vesterlind's reference list) — same supervisor, directly on point for natural-language explanation of football ML.

### 2.8 Expert / domain validation of discovered styles

**TacticAI is the exemplar and our template** (design fully described in §1.7 above; the key parameters worth restating as a checklist):
- **Panel**: 5 experts from a single elite club, deliberately heterogeneous in role (3 data scientists, 1 video analyst, 1 coaching assistant).
- **Blinding**: task details withheld from raters in advance; team and player identities never revealed; only positions, velocities and team membership shown.
- **Item counts**: 50 items per task, drawn from data **held out of the training set**.
- **Scoring**: binary (+1/0) for realism and receiver plausibility and retrieval usefulness; ternary (+1 / 0 / −1) for adjustment quality.
- **Bias control**: for the side-by-side adjustment task, "we randomly select a subset of corner setups for which we reverse the two versions, making the TacticAI suggestion the reference corner".
- **Baseline comparison**: retrieval was benchmarked against raw-feature cosine similarity, giving the headline 0.63 vs 0.33 recall.
- **Statistics**: average ratings computed **both per rater and per item**; one-sample t-tests of per-item means against zero (pooled and per rater); z-tests comparing real vs generated distributions; one-way ANOVA + Tukey for inter-rater structure; F-tests for inter-rater agreement.
- **Reporting of rater heterogeneity as a result**, not noise (three rater clusters on receiver prediction).
- **Qualitative harvest**: the paper documents the heuristics and salient features raters described, and reports the two edge cases where usefulness was contingent on unavailable player attributes.

**Bolin & Vesterlind's lighter-weight approach** (also citable, and more realistic for a Master's thesis): repeated **informal consultations** with football data analysts, club-affiliated staff and the academic supervisor throughout the project, feeding back into cluster interpretation; a **formal presentation to a seminar of 50+ practitioners, analysts and researchers**; and **cross-checking cluster interpretations against public/media narratives** of how specific teams played that season (Elfsborg's counter-attacking reputation matching their Counter Attack cluster share) — an approach adapted from Decroos et al. (2018), who "explored validation by comparing data-driven similarities between players with similarities described in public narratives and media accounts". They cite **Akhanli & Hennig (2023)** for the value of qualitative validation in football clustering.

**Tang et al.**: validation is purely by attribute inspection and hand-written interpretability; no expert panel.

**Plakias et al. 2025**: no expert panel, but style labels are anchored to *named tactical traditions from the literature* (traditional British play citing Hughes & Franks 2005 and Mitrotasios et al. 2019; Spanish *furia* citing Vaczi 2015; tiki-taka citing Davies 2013; Sarriball citing Moustakidis et al. 2023) and cross-checked against known team–manager identities. This is a third, cheap validation mode: **face validity against documented football philosophies**.

**Gu et al.**: recommend, at review level, "hybrid approaches that combine machine learning with expert input [that] may bridge the gap between data-driven outputs and applied coaching practice".

### 2.9 Position-specific and player-level handling

Thin, and this matters for us because our unit of analysis is the individual player.
- **Bolin & Vesterlind** is the only paper that produces player-level style-conditional output: player xT summed per offensive style, presented as "**Top 10 Midfielders** with the highest sum of xT from events in offensive playing style 'Established Possession Circulation'" — i.e. position used as a **filter**, not as a normalisation basis. They also define a per-style **involvement rate** = f(player events in that style, team chains in that style, estimated playing-time fraction), and a per-style predicted xT for a hypothetical transfer. No position-specific feature sets, no within-position percentiles.
- **TacticAI** models players as nodes with only **height and weight** as profile features; players are otherwise anonymous and **roles are available in the line-up data but not used** as model input. Receiver prediction is inherently player-level (node classification). Two rater comments in Task 4 could not be resolved because "the TacticAI variant we trained here did not have access to player features beyond height and weight" — an explicit acknowledgement that player profiles matter for tactical fit.
- **Tang et al.** attribute cluster-level xT_add to named individuals post hoc (De Bruyne's line-breaking passes in Situation 16-2; Kane, Bergwijn and Son's carries; Gabriel Magalhães and Xhaka's left-flank passes; Saka and White's right-flank carries) — but the model itself has no player identities.
- **Gu et al.** flag the relevant prior work without covering it: Plakias et al. (2023) "extended their research to focus on individual player styles, categorizing **26 distinct profiles** into an integrated framework that facilitates player classification and comparison". This is the player-style taxonomy we should chase.
- **Plakias et al. 2025** cite, in its reference list, **Li, Y., Zong, S., Shen, Y., Pu, Z., Gómez, M.-Á., & Cui, Y. (2022). "Characterizing player's playing styles based on player vectors for each playing position in the Chinese Football Super League." Journal of Sports Sciences, 40(14), 1629–1640** — explicitly *per playing position*. Chase this.
- **Zhong, Baattite**: purely team-level. No player modelling.

**Conclusion:** position-aware player style modelling exists in the literature (Plakias et al.'s 26 player profiles; Li et al.'s positional player vectors; Decroos & Davis's Player Vectors) but **none of the seven supplied papers implements it**, and none links a position-aware player representation to a manager- or system-level profile.

## 3. Gap analysis

### 3.1 What the literature already does well — be generous here

1. **Team-level playing-style identification is thoroughly well-trodden.** Gu et al. synthesise 25 empirical studies; Plakias et al. (2023b), cited within, reviewed 50 more. The methodological playbook is settled: many performance indicators → PCA/factor analysis (Varimax, Kaiser, loadings ≥0.6) → interpretable tactical factors → clustering (k-means or Ward) → named styles. This is done. **We must not present team-level style identification as our contribution.**
2. **A validated, published tactical vocabulary exists.** Plakias et al. (2025) supplies 19 bipolar game-situation dimensions covering all four moments of play, with 90.8–100% discriminant classification, applied to 174 teams across 11 leagues. Plakias et al.'s grounded theory (2025, IJSSC) is an explicit attempt at a consensus taxonomy of team playing styles.
3. **Style-conditional performance analysis exists.** Zhong et al. show that KPI→outcome relationships invert between possession and counterattacking systems, with SHAP explanations.
4. **Style-to-style interaction analysis exists.** Bolin & Vesterlind link each possession chain's offensive style to the opposing defensive style, enabling matchup analysis — which they believe is novel at chain level.
5. **Team-level style profiles ("DNA") and their use for benchmarking, opponent scouting and player recruitment are all articulated.** Gu et al. (practical application iii), Plakias et al. 2025 (Strengths and Practical Applications), and Bolin & Vesterlind (four worked applications) all say so.
6. **Player→club style-fit has been prototyped.** Bolin & Vesterlind's transfer model predicts a player's xT at a new club from the club's style distribution and the player's per-style involvement rate and mean xT.
7. **Rigorous expert validation of AI tactical output has been demonstrated.** TacticAI's four-task blinded case study with five Liverpool FC experts is a fully worked, statistically analysed template.
8. **Situation- and context-conditional evaluation exists.** Tang et al.'s Situational xT explicitly replaces location-based states with tactical-situation states.

### 3.2 What appears NOT to be done

**(a) Manager-level philosophy modelling — partially addressed, and one close precedent exists.**
- None of the seven papers models a *manager* as the unit of analysis. Plakias et al. 2025 comes closest by *observing* that Guardiola- and Sarri-influenced teams (Man City, Bayern, Barcelona, Napoli, Chelsea, Lazio, Juventus, Arsenal-under-Arteta) co-cluster in Style F and that Liverpool sits with Atalanta in Style E — but the unit is the team-season, managers are discussed only interpretively, and no manager profile is estimated. Gu et al. mention monitoring "shifts in a team's playing style—whether due to new coaching philosophies or changes in squad composition" as an application, not a model.
- **However: a direct precedent for manager-level style modelling exists in the literature the corpus cites.** Bolin & Vesterlind report that "**Cintia & Pappalardo (2021) used event data, extracted possession chains and clustered similar possessions with K-Means clustering to analyze typical playing styles of different coaches**" (p. 9) and again that they aimed at "creating playing styles for different coaches and evaluating the relative similarities between coaches" (p. 9). The exact reference as printed in their bibliography is: **Cintia, P., & Pappalardo, L. (2021). *Coach2vec: Autoencoding the playing style of soccer coaches.* https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2106.15444**. We have not read this paper and must not characterise it beyond what Bolin & Vesterlind report — but intellectual honesty requires that we acknowledge it as prior art on coach-level style representation, and read it before finalising our novelty claim.
- **Also relevant**: Zhong et al.'s reference list contains **Buldu JM, Busquets J, Echegoyen I, Seirul Lo F. "Defining a historic football team: Using Network Science to analyze Guardiola's F.C. Barcelona." Sci Rep. 2019;9(1):13602** — a manager-specific study.
- What remains genuinely unaddressed: **a manager profile estimated across multiple clubs/seasons in a shared, interpretable tactical-dimension space**, which is what would let us separate the manager's philosophy from any single squad's characteristics. Plakias et al. 2025's own first stated limitation is that a single season "may not capture temporal fluctuations in team behavior due to changes in coaching staff, player roster, or competition strategy" — i.e. they identify the confound we would be resolving.

**(b) Player→manager compatibility — not addressed as a model.**
- Every relevant paper proposes it as an *implication*. Gu et al.: "Agents and analysts can recommend players whose individual characteristics align with a team's dominant playing style" (p. 18). Plakias et al. 2025: "scouting departments and player agents can assess player–team fit" by "matching individual strengths with the tactical requirements of each style" (p. 2255).
- The only *implemented* fit model is Bolin & Vesterlind's, and it is: (i) player→**club**, not player→manager; (ii) a deterministic arithmetic projection, not a learned compatibility model; (iii) evaluated only against a single anecdote (Bergström, Mjällby→Djurgården); (iv) built on a single output metric (xT) rather than a multi-dimensional tactical fit; and (v) explicitly hedged by its authors — "this calculation entails a number of limiting assumptions such as that the players involvement rate for each style will be the same in the new team".
- TacticAI's raters independently surfaced the need: two adjustments were judged only "possibly useful" *depending on the adjusted player's profile*, because "certain runs were only deemed likely to succeed if performed by defenders of high physical capability or fitness" — and the model could not represent this. That is a documented, expert-voiced demand for player-attribute-aware tactical fit.
- **So: player→manager compatibility, estimated in an explainable multi-dimensional tactical space, is not done in this corpus.**

**(c) An explicit knowledge/ontology layer — not addressed.**
- No paper in the corpus builds a formal, documented mapping from raw metrics → football concepts → tactical dimensions. The closest approximations are: Bolin & Vesterlind's grouping of features into named **tactical categories** (General / Passing Patterns / Passing Locations / Team Movement / Pressure; Marking-Zonal / Pressure / Compactness / Positioning) — a two-level proto-ontology with no formal semantics; Plakias et al.'s bipolar game-situation dimensions — a well-specified *dimension* tier without an explicit metric→dimension mapping (the mapping is implicit in factor loadings from the earlier 2023 paper); and the Plakias et al. grounded theory (2025, IJSSC), described by Zhong et al. as having "reviewed and integrated over 200 academic sources to produce a taxonomy of playing styles grounded in empirical literature" — a taxonomy, not a computational layer.
- Gu et al. name this absence as the field's core conceptual problem: no universally accepted definition, "The current use of 'style' remains largely informal, lacking standardized indicators to support systematic evaluation" (p. 17), and their recommendation is a shared operational definition plus hybrid ML + expert-input approaches.
- Conversely, TacticAI is the corpus's strongest **knowledge-light** result and states it "incorporates minimal domain knowledge, and does not rely on intricate feature engineering". Our claim must therefore be carefully scoped: the knowledge layer's value is **explainability, transferability of vocabulary, and data efficiency under small samples** — not raw predictive superiority.

**(d) Secondary gaps we can also claim, each with a citation.**
- **No exposure-normalisation standard** (§2.3): no per-90, possession-adjusted, per-100-passes, or within-position percentile normalisation anywhere in the corpus.
- **No cluster-stability assessment** anywhere in the corpus (§2.5).
- **Multi-season style stability untested**: Plakias et al. 2025 limitation 1; Bolin & Vesterlind §7.2.3.4; Zhong et al.'s unmodelled nesting.
- **Position-aware player representation not linked to system-level style** (§2.9).
- **XAI is nearly absent**: SHAP appears in 1 of 7 papers; permutation importance in 0 of 7; GAT attention available but unused in TacticAI.

### 3.3 A sober statement of our research gap and contribution

> Team-level playing-style identification in football is a mature research area: a 2025 systematic review of 25 studies (Gu et al., 2025) shows a settled methodological pipeline — dimensionality reduction of performance indicators into interpretable tactical factors, followed by clustering — and Plakias et al. (2025) provide a validated, soccer-specific set of 19 bipolar tactical dimensions that classify 174 European teams into four interpretable overall styles with 90.8–100% discriminant accuracy. Style-conditional performance modelling is also established: Zhong et al. (2026) demonstrate with SHAP that the same key performance indicator can be positively associated with winning under one style and negatively under another. Downstream, the literature repeatedly *proposes* using style profiles for player recruitment (Gu et al., 2025; Plakias et al., 2025), and one Master's thesis prototypes a player→club style-fit projection (Bolin & Vesterlind, 2025).
>
> Three things remain undone in this literature. First, the unit of analysis is the **team-season**, not the **manager**: Plakias et al. (2025) observe that Guardiola-lineage teams co-cluster and that Liverpool clusters with Atalanta, but they estimate no manager profile, and their own first stated limitation is that single-season team data cannot separate coaching philosophy from squad composition. We acknowledge that coach-level style representation is not wholly unexplored — Cintia & Pappalardo's *Coach2vec* (arXiv:2106.15444) clusters possession chains to characterise coaches — and our claim is therefore not the invention of manager-level style modelling but its estimation across clubs and seasons within a **published, interpretable, bipolar tactical-dimension vocabulary** rather than an opaque embedding. Second, **player→manager compatibility is proposed everywhere and implemented nowhere** as an explainable multi-dimensional model: the one existing fit model is a deterministic single-metric (xT) projection of a player onto a club's style distribution, validated against a single anecdote. Third, **no paper in this corpus makes the metric → football-concept → tactical-dimension mapping explicit and auditable**; the closest precedents are an informal grouping of features into tactical categories (Bolin & Vesterlind, 2025) and a literature-derived taxonomy (Plakias et al.'s grounded theory), while the corpus's most advanced system deliberately "incorporates minimal domain knowledge" (TacticAI).
>
> **Our contribution is therefore an integration rather than a new algorithm, and should be claimed as such:** an explicit, auditable Football Knowledge Layer that maps raw metrics to football concepts and then to the Plakias et al. (2025) tactical dimensions; the estimation of **manager-level** philosophy profiles in that shared space for three contrasting elite managers; a **player→manager compatibility** estimate expressed dimension-by-dimension rather than as a single opaque score; and **explanations in tactical language** (SHAP over knowledge-layer features, plus per-dimension gap reporting) validated by a blinded expert protocol adapted from TacticAI's four-task case study. Secondary methodological contributions, each addressing a documented field-level weakness, are: full PCA/clustering reporting against the Rojas-Valverde et al. (2021) 21-item checklist (Gu et al. report that only 22% of clustering studies justify their choice of k and only 64% report Bartlett's test); a formal cluster-stability assessment (absent from all 25 reviewed studies and all seven papers read here); and explicit, justified exposure normalisation (no per-90, possession-adjusted or within-position percentile normalisation appears anywhere in this corpus).
>
> We explicitly do **not** claim that knowledge-informed ML outperforms knowledge-light deep learning on predictive tasks; TacticAI is evidence to the contrary in its domain. We claim interpretability, auditability, and the ability to answer a question — *which manager's philosophy does this player fit, and why* — that the existing pipelines cannot answer in tactical language.

## 4. Recommendations to the project

### 4.1 Features and dimensions to adopt, with the citation that justifies each

**Tier 3 — Tactical dimensions (adopt wholesale):**
- **Adopt the 19 bipolar game-situation dimensions of Plakias et al. (2025), Table 1**, verbatim including both poles. Justification: validated on 174 teams / 11 leagues / 5,998 team-match observations, with discriminant classification rates of 90.8–100%; covers all four moments of play; already used to identify Guardiola- and Klopp-associated styles. Watch the reversed polarity of *Passing tempo* (#16) and *Defending aggressively* (#17).
- **Extend with three tracking-derived dimensions absent from that set**: (i) **team compactness/shape** — Bolin & Vesterlind's Vertical Compactness, Horizontal Compactness and convex-hull Team Area (citing Forcher et al. 2024a and Martín-Castellanos et al. 2024 for "team average length"/"team average width"); (ii) **marking scheme, man-oriented vs zonal** — Bolin & Vesterlind's Defensive Proximity, which they state is novel and expert-motivated; (iii) **physical/high-intensity output** — Gu et al.'s tabulated HIRD, HIRE, HSRD, HSRE, SprintD, SprintE, TD (from Kong et al., Zhou et al., Ruan et al., Martín-Castellanos et al.).
- **Represent each manager as a position on each dimension, and gate what counts as "defining" by effect size.** Justification: Plakias et al. (2025) built their published style descriptions only from differences with Cohen's d > 0.8 (thresholds 0.2/0.5/0.8/1.2/2.0).

**Tier 1–2 — Raw metrics and football concepts (build from these catalogues):**
- **If event data only**: adopt Baattite's 38 KPI definitions as the starting dictionary — they come with explicit computational rules in StatsBomb coordinates (possession by third and by channel; pass directness from length and angle; forward/sideways/backward by 45–135° bands; passes defence→middle/attack; crosses and early crosses as % of attacking sequences; shots inside/outside box; ball recoveries by third and channel; tempo as directness × pass duration; PPDA for pressing intensity; counter-press within 5 s of turnover; through passes; cut-backs; Work Ball Into Box; team mentality; set-piece efficiency).
- **Cross-check against Zhong et al.'s 30 WhoScored/OPTA variables** (full list in §1.3) for provider availability, and against Gu et al.'s per-study variable inventory (§1.1) for coverage by dimension.
- **If tracking data is available**: adopt Bolin & Vesterlind's 21 event-level features with their formulas (§1.4), each with a supporting citation already identified — Andrienko et al. (2018) for distance-decay pressure; Herold et al. (2022) for banded multi-radius pressure; Forcher et al. (2024a) for convex hull, numerical superiority around the ball, and closest-defender pressing; Casey-Popovich (2024) for opponents behind the ball; Fernandez-Navarro et al. Justification: Bolin & Vesterlind use avg xT per style, per-player summed xT per style, and xT in their transfer model; Tang et al. build Situational xT. ### 4.3 Pitfalls the literature warns about that we must avoid

1. **Do not treat possession % (or any volume metric) as a style label.** Zhong et al.: "possession rate provides only a basic classification and oversimplifies the complexity of playing styles by focusing solely on ball control".
2. **Do not assume a metric has a fixed valence.** Zhong et al.'s core result: crosses, key passes, possession, cards per foul all flip sign between styles. A compatibility model that scores players on universally "good" metrics is therefore wrong by construction.
3. **Do not select k on internal indices alone, and do not silently omit the k rule.** Gu et al.: only 2 of 9 clustering studies reported a criterion. Zhong et al.'s silhouette-optimal k was 2 — which Bolin & Vesterlind argue "risked oversimplifying the tactical diversity present in the data".
4. **Do not over-interpret low silhouette scores as failure, but do not hide them either.** Bolin & Vesterlind's §7.1.4 gives us the argument; use it transparently rather than omitting the numbers.
5. **Beware outcome bias in clustering.** Bolin & Vesterlind: stylistically identical sequences can be split into different clusters purely by endpoint, producing "a subtle spatial-success bias". If our manager profiles are built from possessions or matches, successful and failed executions of the same philosophy must not be assigned to different philosophies.
6. **Beware hard assignment.** Bolin & Vesterlind: K-Means "assumes that clusters are roughly spherical and of similar size… playing styles are often blended and fluid, and a single possession chain might exhibit characteristics of more than one tactical archetype". Our compatibility output should be **continuous and multi-dimensional**, not a single hard label.
7. **Beware pitch-zone contextual bias in features.** Bolin & Vesterlind: "high pressure" means different things in one's own build-up zone and in the opponent's box. Consider phase- or zone-conditional definitions or normalisation.
8. **Beware degenerate and compositional features.** Bolin & Vesterlind found Vertical Switch showed "almost no variation" and "likely contributed little to the clustering". Baattite's Factor 5 is defined by RCA (−0.96) and RWA (+0.96) — two shares that sum to 1, so the factor may be an artefact of a compositional constraint. Screen for near-zero variance and for complementary-share pairs.
9. **Beware pressure metrics that ignore velocity.** Bolin & Vesterlind, from expert feedback: a defender 7 m away sprinting at the ball may exert more pressure than one 3 m away standing still. If we build pressure features, include velocity or state the limitation.
10. **Beware nesting / non-independence.** Zhong et al.: repeated team observations across seasons "may violate independence assumptions"; they recommend hierarchical or mixed-effects models. Our manager profiles will pool many matches per team-season — acknowledge or model this.
11. **Do not over-filter silently.** Bolin & Vesterlind removed 53.7% of possession chains and note that sequences containing goals may be lost. Their own mitigation suggestion is good practice: retain and *label* excluded data so profiles show where information is missing.
12. **Do not evaluate primarily on match-outcome prediction.** Baattite's best regression achieved R² = 0.23 and best classifier 0.75 test accuracy; Zhong et al.'s best AUC was 0.783. Style→outcome signal is weak; our evaluation should rest on interpretability, expert validation and construct validity instead.
13. **Do not overfit small style-score datasets with tree ensembles.** Baattite: Decision Tree 1.00 train / 0.61 test; Random Forest, Extra Trees, Bagging all ~1.00 / ~0.70.
14. **Do not overclaim novelty.** Plakias et al. 2025 already cluster Guardiola-lineage teams together; Cintia & Pappalardo already model coaches; Bolin & Vesterlind already prototype player→club style fit; Gu et al. and Plakias et al. already propose style-based recruitment. Our novelty is integration, manager-level abstraction across clubs/seasons, explicit ontology, and explanation — nothing more.
15. **Do not claim a definition of playing style that the field has settled.** Gu et al.: "the absence of a universally accepted definition impedes cross-study comparisons"; Bolin & Vesterlind: "It is unlikely that any universally applicable taxonomy will emerge that satisfies all analytical, coaching, and theoretical perspectives". State our operational definition and its provenance, and treat it as a choice rather than a truth.
16. **Beware the interpretability/accuracy trade-off, and say which side we chose and why.** Bolin & Vesterlind (citing Lipton 2018 and Sun et al. 2020) and Zhong et al. (arguing for autoencoders over PCA) take opposite sides. Our project's answer is interpretability, and the argument must be made explicitly rather than assumed.
